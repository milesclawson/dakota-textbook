Dakhotiyapi · Chapter 2

Óta na Owáŋži

Many and One — Numbers, Colors & Descriptors
← Back to Chapter 2
Setting the Scene

The family is preparing for a community giveaway — wópila, a feast of gratitude. Wakíŋyaŋ is put to work counting supplies and sorting cloth by color. He quickly learns that in Dakota, colors are not just adjectives — they are stative verbs. A thing doesn't have a color; it is that color.

1 Counting the Supplies

Aunt hands Wakíŋyaŋ a list: waŋží, núŋpa, yámni, tópa, záptaŋ — one, two, three, four, five. He counts blankets, then tins of dried meat. The numbers feel rhythmic, almost like a song.

2 Colors Are Actions

He picks up a red cloth. His cousin corrects him: don't say "the red cloth," say šá — it is red. Tó means it is blue. Zí means it is yellow. In Dakota, color lives in the verb, not the noun.

3 Six Through Ten

That evening grandmother drills him on the higher numbers: šákpe, šakówiŋ, šaglóğaŋ, napčíyuŋka, wikčémna — six through ten. Wikčémna means ten, but its root is "knuckle-full" — an old counting method using hands.

4 The Wópila Table

The giveaway table is covered with gifts. Wakíŋyaŋ counts aloud as he places them: Núŋpa, yámni, tópa… Two, three, four. The elders nod. Speaking numbers out loud is part of doing them right.

5 How Many? How Much?

His aunt asks: Tóna he? — How many? He answers: Wikčémna tópa — forty (four tens). He is starting to see how Dakota builds large numbers from small ones — tens multiplied, then added.

6 Describing the Sky

The sunset turns the clouds orange and purple. Gí — it is brown/orange. Sáŋ — it is whitish-grey. Wakíŋyaŋ looks at the sky and narrates it in Dakota, one color-verb at a time.

7 The Gift Count

At the ceremony, an elder announces: Wikčémna šákpe na tópa — sixty-four gifts — have been given today. The crowd murmurs approval. Generosity is counted not hoarded.

8 Numbers in the Land

Grandmother explains: the Dakota number system grew from the body and the land — knuckles, fingers, winters. When you say wikčémna, you are saying something about how Dakota people understood the world.

You Can Do This Too

  • Count ten objects around you in Dakota — out loud.
  • Look at three things in the room. Use color-stative verbs: "Šá," "Tó," "Sáŋ."
  • Ask a classmate "Tóna he?" about something countable.
  • Write out 11, 15, 20, and 64 in Dakota words.
