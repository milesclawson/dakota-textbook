Makóčhe Kiŋ

The Land — Seasons, Directions & Sacred Geography
← Back to Chapter 6
Setting the Scene

Late summer. Wakíŋyaŋ goes on a four-day journey with his uncle along the Minnesota River — what Dakota people call the Mnísota Wakpá. His uncle teaches navigation and seasonal knowledge entirely in Dakota, naming each direction, each moon, each change in the land.

1 Setting Out

They leave at dawn heading east — Wiyóhiŋyaŋpata — toward where the sun rises. Uncle points to each direction: Wazíyata north, Itókagata south, Wiyóhpeyata west. Wakíŋyaŋ repeats each one facing that direction.

2 The River Has a Name

Mnísota Wakpá — smoky or cloudy water river. Mní is water. Sota means smoke-clouded. Every place name tells you something about the land — what it looks like, what happened there, who it belongs to.

3 The Four Seasons

Uncle names the seasons: Waniyetu — winter; Wétu — spring; Bdokétu — summer; Ptaŋyétu — fall. Each name describes the feel of the time: waniyetu contains the word for cold and for year.

4 The Moon Calendar

The full moon rises. Uncle tells him its name: Wasútoŋ Wi — the Moon When Corn Ripens (August). Each moon has a name tied to what the land and community are doing. Time is not a clock — it is an ecological story.

5 Bdóte — The Meeting of Waters

They reach the confluence of the Minnesota and Mississippi rivers. Uncle says quietly: Bdóte. The meeting place. This is the center of the Dakota world — where the Mdewakanton originated. Wakíŋyaŋ stands at the edge and feels the weight of the word.

6 Talking About Weather

A thunderstorm rolls in from the west. Wakinyan kiŋ hí. — The thunderbirds are coming. Mahpíya sápa. — The sky is black. The word wakinyan — thunderbird — is the same sound as his own name. He had not noticed until now.

7 Returning Home

On the fourth day they return. Uncle says: Makóčhe kiŋ waúŋkičuŋpi. — We carry the land with us. Land in Dakota is not owned — it is held in relationship. The language does not have a word for "my land" the way English does.

8 The Land Speaks

Every Dakota place name is also a teaching about that place. When the English names replaced Dakota ones, something about the land was erased. Wakíŋyaŋ vows to learn as many place names as he can. The land has been speaking all along.
