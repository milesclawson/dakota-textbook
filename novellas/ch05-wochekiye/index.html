Dakhotiyapi · Chapter 5

Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ

All My Relations — Values, Prayer & the Sacred in Dakota
← Back to Chapter 5
Setting the Scene

Summer arrives. The tiyóšpaye gathers for a ceremony honoring those who have passed. Wakíŋyaŋ is asked to help prepare the prayer space. He learns that the most important Dakota phrases are not everyday words — they are expressions of relationship, gratitude, and the sacred.

1 The Circle Forms

Elders begin to form a circle as the fire is lit. Someone begins a low song. Wakíŋyaŋ sits at the edge and listens. He catches one phrase repeated again and again: Mitákuye oyásʼiŋ — all my relations.

2 What "All My Relations" Means

Mitákuye oyásʼiŋ does not mean family alone. It means every being — people, animals, plants, sky, water. When you say it, you are saying: I know that I am not alone and never have been.

3 The Four Virtues

Grandmother names the four Dakota virtues: wóohola — respect; wóčhantognaka — generosity; wówaúŋšila — compassion; wóksape — wisdom. She says: these are not rules. They are a direction for living.

4 Prayer and Language

The ceremony begins with words Wakíŋyaŋ does not fully understand, but he understands the tone — careful, slow, grateful. His uncle tells him: prayer in Dakota was never asking for things. Wóčhekiye — prayer — means "to speak in relationship with."

5 Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka

Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka — Great Mystery, or Great Spirit. Wakȟáŋ means sacred, mysterious, powerful. Tȟáŋka means great or large. Wakíŋyaŋ writes it down. It is not a proper noun in the Western sense — it describes a quality of all things.

6 Speaking with Care

After the ceremony, grandmother explains: some words should only be said at the right time, in the right place. Čhaŋtéčhiyuza — hold a ceremony / hold it in your heart. Language is not decoration; it has power.

7 Giving Thanks

The wópila feast begins. Wakíŋyaŋ says to each elder: Pidámayaye. — Thank you. An elder responds: Léčhaŋl ečhúŋhpi kiŋ waštéčhila. — I love what we do here. The phrase carries three verbs at once.

8 Carrying the Words

That night Wakíŋyaŋ sits and holds the phrase Mitákuye oyásʼiŋ in his mind. It does not feel like a foreign word anymore. It feels like a door. Every language has words that are also whole philosophies.
