Setting the Scene

Wakíŋyaŋ's grandmother falls ill with a cold. He must help communicate with the visiting nurse and also care for her at home. Through worry and care, he discovers that Dakota body vocabulary runs deep — the body and the emotions are not separate worlds.

1 Grandmother Is Unwell

Grandmother coughs. Čhapúŋpuŋ mayázaŋ.

Čhapúŋpuŋ mayázaŋ.

📖 Dictionary
🔊 = synthesized speech · Dictionary = recorded native speaker
 is the verb "I am in pain / I am sick." Wakíŋyaŋ realizes that illness in Dakota is expressed as a verb, not a noun. She is doing sickness.

3 The Heart Word

Čhaŋté mayázaŋ

Čhaŋté mayázaŋ
chaŋ-TAY mah-YAH-zhaŋ
My heart aches
📖 Dictionary
🔊 = synthesized speech · Dictionary = recorded native speaker
— my heart aches. He says it without thinking, not about a physical pain but about worry. Grandmother looks up and says: that is correct — we say it the same way for both.

You Can Do This Too

  • Learn the body parts: point to pa, išta, noge, čhaŋté, and say each aloud.
  • Ask a partner "Tókhel yaúŋ he?" and practice one answer.
  • Use mayázaŋ in a sentence about something that bothers you (real or invented).
  • Look up one more kinship term (father, sibling, cousin) in Dakota.
