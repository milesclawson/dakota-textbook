Dakhotiyapi · Chapter 4

Čhanté Mayázaŋ

My Heart Aches — Body, Health & Kinship in Dakota
← Back to Chapter 4
Setting the Scene

Wakíŋyaŋ's grandmother falls ill with a cold. He must help communicate with the visiting nurse and also care for her at home. Through worry and care, he discovers that Dakota body vocabulary runs deep — the body and the emotions are not separate worlds.

1 Grandmother Is Unwell

Grandmother coughs. Čhapúŋpuŋ mayázaŋ. — I am sick with a cough. Mayázaŋ is the verb "I am in pain / I am sick." Wakíŋyaŋ realizes that illness in Dakota is expressed as a verb, not a noun. She is doing sickness.

2 Body Parts — Head to Foot

Pa — head. Išta — eye. Noge — ear. Čhaŋté — heart. As he names each part, grandmother corrects his pronunciation gently. The Dakota word for vein shares its root with the word for road — the body has its own paths.

3 The Heart Word

Čhaŋté mayázaŋ — my heart aches. He says it without thinking, not about a physical pain but about worry. Grandmother looks up and says: that is correct — we say it the same way for both.

4 Kinship Terms

The visiting nurse asks who Wakíŋyaŋ is. He explains in Dakota: Uŋčí kiŋ hé miyé. — That is my grandmother. He learns that kinship terms in Dakota change depending on whether you are male or female — his grandmother calls him čhiŋkší, her son's child.

5 Asking After Health

A neighbor arrives: Tókhel yaúŋ he? — How are you? Grandmother answers: Hé! Čhapúŋpuŋ mayázaŋ tka oká waúŋ. — Hmm! I'm sick with a cough but I'm managing. The word oká waúŋ — I'm getting by — carries quiet dignity.

6 Medicine and Care

Wakíŋyaŋ makes soup. Čhaŋpásapa wóžapi — chokecherry soup — is grandmother's remedy. He hears the word wóžapi and realizes it's a verb-noun: a thing-you-stew. Dakota often builds nouns from cooking verbs.

7 She Is Getting Better

Two days later: Waŋná aŋpétu kiŋ wašté. — Now the day is good. Zaniíčhiyapi. — You have gotten well. Wakíŋyaŋ sits beside her, relief flooding his chest. Čhaŋté wašté. — Heart good. He says it as both report and prayer.

8 The Body Speaks

Grandmother tells him: in Dakota thought, čhaŋté is the seat of all wisdom, not the brain. Čhaŋté ičínažiŋ — stand up in your heart — means to be courageous. Learning the language is learning how Dakota people locate the self.

You Can Do This Too

  • Learn the body parts: point to pa, išta, noge, čhaŋté, and say each aloud.
  • Ask a partner "Tókhel yaúŋ he?" and practice one answer.
  • Use mayázaŋ in a sentence about something that bothers you (real or invented).
  • Look up one more kinship term (father, sibling, cousin) in Dakota.
