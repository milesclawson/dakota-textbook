    Chapter 8 Quiz — Oyáte: Nation, Treaties & Sovereignty¶

    Part A — Multiple Choice (10 questions, 2 points each)¶

    1. What does the word oyáte mean in Dakota?
    Answer

    b) Nation, people, community

    oyáte carries the full political weight of "nation" or "people." It is not equivalent to "tribe" (a term with diminishing implications imposed from outside) — it names a sovereign people with their own governance, land, and relationships.

    a) Tribe b) Nation, people, community c) Leader, chief d) Land, territory

    2. What does Očhéthi Šakówiŋ mean literally?
    Answer

    c) Seven Council Fires

    Očhéthi = fireplace/hearth/council fire; šakówiŋ = seven. The name refers to the seven council groups of the Dakota, Nakota, and Lakota peoples who form the broader confederation. Each "fire" represents a distinct but related council group.

    a) Seven Sacred Nations b) Seven Brothers of the Prairie c) Seven Council Fires d) Seven Winter Counts

    3. On December 26, 1862, 38 Dakota men were executed in Mankato. What made this event historically unprecedented?
    Answer

    b) It was the largest mass execution in United States history

    The simultaneous hanging of 38 men on December 26, 1862 in Mankato, Minnesota was and remains the largest mass execution in U.S. history. This followed a military tribunal in which 303 men were originally sentenced to death in trials lasting as little as five minutes.

    a) It was the first execution of Native people under federal law b) It was the largest mass execution in United States history c) It was ordered by Abraham Lincoln personally without any review d) It took place on Dakota sacred ground at Bdóte

    4. Approximately how many Dakota women, children, and elderly were imprisoned in the Fort Snelling internment camp in winter 1862–63?
    Answer

    c) Approximately 1,700

    Following the U.S.-Dakota War, approximately 1,700 Dakota women, children, and elderly were imprisoned below Fort Snelling in a concentration camp during the winter of 1862–63. Many died of disease and exposure. The survivors were then exiled to Crow Creek Reservation in South Dakota.

    a) Approximately 100 b) Approximately 500 c) Approximately 1,700 d) Approximately 5,000

    5. The Dakota word čhaŋtéšiča — used as a name for the 1862 War — means:
    Answer

    b) Heartbreak / sorrow

    čhaŋtéšiča = čhaŋté (heart) + šiča (bad/painful) = heartbreak, sorrow. This is the Dakota people's own name for the conflict of 1862 — not the militarized "War" of U.S. historical naming, but the sorrow that followed a decade of broken promises and starvation.

    a) Time of fire b) Heartbreak / sorrow c) The great battle d) Year of the hanging

    6. What is the Wokiksuye ride, and how often does it occur?
    Answer

    b) An annual 330-mile horseback commemoration of the 38 men hanged in Mankato — held every December since 1987

    The Wokiksuye (remembrance) ride travels approximately 330 miles from Lower Brule, South Dakota to Mankato, Minnesota every December, arriving near the anniversary of the December 26, 1862 executions. It has taken place annually since 1987 and is an act of living political witness.

    a) A weekly ceremony held at Bdóte b) An annual 330-mile horseback commemoration of the 38 men hanged in Mankato — held every December since 1987 c) A biennial gathering of all Seven Council Fires communities d) A monthly language revitalization program in Minneapolis

    7. The Bdewákanthuŋwaŋ (Mdewakanton) people are best described as:
    Answer

    a) People of Spirit Lake — one of the four eastern Dakota council fires

    Bdewákanthuŋwaŋ = Bdewákaŋ (Spirit Lake/Mille Lacs) + thuŋwaŋ (people of). They are the People of Spirit Lake, one of the four eastern Dakota fires. The Mdewakanton communities today include Prairie Island, Shakopee, Upper Sioux, and Lower Sioux in Minnesota.

    a) People of Spirit Lake — one of the four eastern Dakota council fires b) People of the Prairie — the westernmost Lakota council fire c) People of the Swampy Land — a northern Dakota group d) Shooters Among the Leaves — a southern Dakota group

    8. After the 1863 exile, Dakota people were forced to relocate to which reservation, where hundreds more died?
    Answer

    c) Crow Creek Reservation, South Dakota

    Survivors of the Fort Snelling internment were exiled to Crow Creek Reservation in South Dakota — an arid, resource-poor location entirely unlike the Minnesota homeland. Hundreds died of disease, starvation, and exposure in the first years at Crow Creek.

    a) Pine Ridge Reservation b) Standing Rock Reservation c) Crow Creek Reservation, South Dakota d) White Earth Reservation, Minnesota

    9. The Dakota word wóokihi means:
    Answer

    b) Ability, power, sovereignty

    wóokihi means the capacity or power to act — sovereignty in the sense of self-governance and self-determination. It is distinct from political sovereignty as a legal concept; it expresses the lived capacity of a people to determine their own path.

    a) Prayer, ceremony b) Ability, power, sovereignty c) Story, narrative d) Treaty, agreement

    10. According to the chapter, how many of the original 303 condemned men had their sentences commuted before the December 26, 1862 execution?
    Answer

    b) 265 (reducing the count from 303 to 38)

    President Lincoln reviewed the 303 military tribunal sentences and commuted all but 38. The review was itself controversial — conducted rapidly under enormous political pressure from Minnesota officials and settlers who wanted all executions carried out. 265 sentences were commuted; 38 men were executed.

    a) 200 (leaving 103 condemned) b) 265 (reducing the count from 303 to 38) c) 290 (leaving 13 condemned) d) 300 (leaving 3 condemned)

    Part B — Fill in the Blank (3 questions, 5 points each)¶

    11. The largest mass execution in U.S. history took place on December 26, _, in _, Minnesota.
    Answer
    • Year: 1862
    • City: Mankato

    38 Dakota men were hanged simultaneously in Mankato, Minnesota on December 26, 1862. This followed a military tribunal in which 303 men were sentenced to death in trials averaging five minutes. President Lincoln commuted all but 38 sentences.

    12. The Dakota phrase meaning 'The Dakota language lives' is: Dakota iápi kiŋ ____.
    Answer

    wičhoni

    Dakota iápi kiŋ wičhoni. — The Dakota language lives. wičhoni = life, living. This phrase is used in language revitalization contexts as an affirmation of the language's survival and ongoing presence.

    13. The Seven Council Fires are collectively known in Dakota as ____.
    Answer

    Očhéthi Šakówiŋ

    Očhéthi = council fire/hearth; šakówiŋ = seven. The Očhéthi Šakówiŋ comprises the seven related council groups of the Dakota (šakówiŋ = seven), Nakota, and Lakota peoples. All seven share the Lakȟótiyapi/Dakhótiyapi language family.

    Part C — Short Answer (3 questions, 10 points each)¶

    14. Describe the sequence of events from the signing of the 1851 treaties through the 1863 exile of Dakota people. What role did the withholding of annuities play in causing the U.S.-Dakota War?
    Rubric / Sample Answer

    Full credit (10 pts): Student provides an accurate chronological sequence covering: 1851 treaties (land cession, promised annuities), withheld annuities, starvation conditions by 1862, the war (August 1862), the military tribunal (303 sentenced), Lincoln's commutation (38 remain), Mankato executions (December 26, 1862), Fort Snelling internment (1862–63), and Crow Creek exile (1863). Student clearly explains the role of withheld annuities in creating the starvation conditions that catalyzed the war.

    Partial credit (5–7 pts): Accurate but incomplete sequence; may miss Crow Creek or Fort Snelling.

    Sample answer: "The 1851 Treaties of Traverse des Sioux and Mendota stripped Dakota people of most of their Minnesota homeland in exchange for a narrow reservation along the Minnesota River and annual annuity payments. Almost immediately, these annuities were withheld, delayed, or diverted by corrupt agents. By 1861–62, reservation conditions were desperate — people were starving. When Dakota leaders attempted to access food stores, trader Andrew Myrick reportedly said 'Let them eat grass.' In August 1862, Dakota warriors led by Little Crow attacked U.S. settlements along the Minnesota River, beginning the U.S.-Dakota War. After the Dakota forces surrendered following the Battle of Wood Lake in September, a military tribunal sentenced 303 men to death in trials averaging five minutes each. President Lincoln reviewed the sentences and commuted all but 38. On December 26, 1862 — the largest mass execution in U.S. history — 38 Dakota men were hanged simultaneously in Mankato. That same winter, approximately 1,700 Dakota women, children, and elderly were imprisoned below Fort Snelling, where dozens died of disease and exposure. In 1863, surviving Dakota people were expelled from Minnesota, with a state bounty placed on Dakota scalps, and the survivors were exiled to the desolate Crow Creek Reservation in South Dakota, where hundreds more died."

    15. Explain the meaning of wokiksuye — remembrance — and describe the Wokiksuye ride. Why is this act of annual commemoration also an act of political sovereignty?
    Rubric / Sample Answer

    Full credit (10 pts): Student accurately breaks down wokiksuye morphologically (or describes its meaning), describes the annual 330-mile ride from Lower Brule to Mankato, and articulates why annual commemoration is a political act — asserting continuity, refusing erasure, making visible the connection between the living and the 38.

    Partial credit (5–7 pts): Accurate description of the ride but shallow analysis of its political dimension.

    Sample answer: "Wokiksuye breaks down as wo- (nominalizer) + -kik- (reflexive/reciprocal) + -suye (to remember). It is a collective self-remembering — the community remembering itself through its dead. Every December since 1987, riders travel approximately 330 miles from Lower Brule, South Dakota to Mankato, Minnesota, arriving near the anniversary of the December 26, 1862 executions. The ride is an act of political sovereignty because it refuses the erasure that the U.S. government attempted in 1862 and after. To say 'we remember' is to assert that the connection between the living Dakota people and the 38 men is unbroken — that the executions did not end the Dakota nation, that the exile did not sever the people from their history. The ride makes this assertion physically, publicly, and annually. It is not nostalgia; it is witness. And it takes place on the land — on the makóčhe — from which the Dakota people were expelled, demonstrating that the relationship to the land also continues."

    16. This is the final chapter of the course. Reflect on the arc of this textbook: from Háu / Haŋ (greetings, Chapter 1) to Oyáte (nation and sovereignty, Chapter 8). How has learning the Dakota language changed or deepened your understanding of Dakota history, culture, and the meaning of language itself? Reference at least three specific concepts or vocabulary items from different chapters in your answer.
    Rubric / Sample Answer

    Full credit (10 pts): Student reflects substantively on personal growth across the course, demonstrates genuine engagement with at least three specific concepts from different chapters, and connects language learning to the broader themes of sovereignty, relationship, and resistance that the course has developed. Reflection shows critical thinking, not just summary.

    Partial credit (5–7 pts): Student references course content but reflection is primarily summary rather than synthesis; or fewer than three chapters represented.

    Sample answer: "Beginning this course with Háu / Haŋ — two different greetings depending on the speaker's gender — I thought I was learning a vocabulary distinction. By the end, I understand I was encountering a whole philosophy of relationship: that who you are shapes how you speak, and how you speak shapes who you are in relation to others. In Chapter 2, learning that color words are stative verbs — that 'red' is something a thing is in, not something it has — changed how I understood descriptive language itself. In Chapter 5, learning that Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ is a personal declaration of kinship with all living beings taught me that Dakota spirituality is not a religion compartmentalized from life but a grammar of relationship that infuses everything. And in Chapter 8, learning the word wokiksuye — remembrance as collective self-memory, not nostalgia — taught me that language revitalization is not an academic preservation project. It is the active refusal of erasure. Every Dakota word I learn connects me, however distantly, to a chain of transmission that was nearly destroyed and is still being rebuilt. That is not a neutral thing. It is a responsibility."
