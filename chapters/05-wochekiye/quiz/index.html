    Chapter 5 Quiz — Wóčhekiye: Prayer, Values & The Sacred¶

    Part A — Multiple Choice (10 questions, 2 points each)¶

    1. What is the most accurate English translation of Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ?
    Answer

    b) All my relations / we are all related

    Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ breaks down as mi- (my) + tákuye (relations) + oyásʼiŋ (all). It is a personal declaration of kinship with all living beings. "All my relations" captures the possessive mi- better than "all relations" does.

    a) I am sacred b) All my relations / we are all related c) Great Spirit, hear me d) We are one people

    2. The Dakota word wakan is best translated as:
    Answer

    c) Sacred, mysterious, charged with power

    wakan covers a range English splits into many words: sacred, mysterious, spiritually powerful, beyond ordinary comprehension. It was used for electricity, steam engines, and ceremony alike — anything that exceeds ordinary knowing.

    a) Holy man b) Prayer c) Sacred, mysterious, charged with power d) Ancestor spirit

    3. Which of the four cardinal Dakota virtues is best described as 'expanding one's heart outward toward the community'?
    Answer

    a) wačháŋtognaka (generosity)

    wačháŋtognaka — generosity — is the most socially central virtue. In Dakota culture, the most honored person gave away the most. The word itself contains čhaŋté (heart) and connotes placing one's heart-resources outward toward others.

    a) wačháŋtognaka (generosity) b) ohítika (bravery) c) ksapá (wisdom) d) čhaŋtéčhila (love)

    4. What law, passed in 1883, made many Dakota spiritual practices illegal?
    Answer

    b) The Code of Indian Offenses

    The Code of Indian Offenses (1883) targeted the Sun Dance, traditional ceremony, and the practices of medicine people. Violation could result in imprisonment and withholding of rations. This was reversed by the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978.

    a) The Indian Removal Act b) The Code of Indian Offenses c) The Dawes Act d) The Indian Civilization Act

    5. How do you say 'I will be brave' in Dakota?
    Answer

    c) Ohítika waúŋ kte.

    ohítika (bravery/brave, stative verb) + waúŋ (I am/exist) + kte (future suffix) = "I will be in a state of bravery." The stative verb pattern requires waúŋ to link the virtue to the first person.

    a) Ohítika yá kte. b) Ohítika waúŋ šni. c) Ohítika waúŋ kte. d) Waúŋ ohítika kte.

    6. What is inípi?
    Answer

    b) The sweat lodge purification ceremony

    inípi is the Dakota/Lakota sweat lodge ceremony, used for purification of body and spirit. It was one of the ceremonies specifically targeted by the 1883 Code of Indian Offenses. iní = to take a sweat bath.

    a) A sacred pipe b) The sweat lodge purification ceremony c) A give-away feast d) A winter prayer ceremony

    7. The word čheyáka refers to which ceremonial plant?
    Answer

    a) Sage

    čheyáka = sage, used in smudging and purification rituals. čhaŋšášayapi or čháŋšaša is kinnikinnick (red willow bark), used in the sacred pipe mixture. These two plants are distinct but both important in ceremony.

    a) Sage b) Cedar c) Sweetgrass d) Red willow

    8. Which word means 'wisdom' as a Dakota cardinal virtue?
    Answer

    c) ksapá

    ksapá = wisdom, specifically practical wisdom earned through experience and listening. It is one of the four cardinal virtues alongside wačháŋtognaka (generosity), ohítika (bravery), and čhaŋtéčhila (love).

    a) wačháŋtognaka b) ohítika c) ksapá d) wóčhekiye

    9. Why is Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka better translated as 'Great Mystery' than 'Great Spirit'?
    Answer

    b) Because wakan means "mysterious/beyond ordinary knowing" — not just "spiritual" — and "Mystery" better captures this sense of the unknowable

    wakȟáŋ covers electricity, steam locomotives, and sacred ceremony — anything that exceeds ordinary comprehension. "Great Spirit" imports a Western personified deity concept that does not match the Dakota philosophical meaning. "Great Mystery" is more philosophically accurate.

    a) Because the Dakota did not believe in spirits b) Because wakan means "mysterious/beyond ordinary knowing" — not just "spiritual" — and "Mystery" better captures this sense of the unknowable c) Because the word tȟáŋka means mystery, not greatness d) Because missionaries introduced the translation "Great Spirit" as a conversion tool and it is therefore colonial

    10. In what year did the American Indian Religious Freedom Act restore legal protections for Native ceremonial practices?
    Answer

    c) 1978

    The American Indian Religious Freedom Act was signed in 1978, nearly a century after the 1883 Code of Indian Offenses criminalized many Dakota and other indigenous spiritual practices. However, enforcement has remained inconsistent since 1978.

    a) 1934 b) 1968 c) 1978 d) 1990

    Part B — Fill in the Blank (3 questions, 5 points each)¶

    11. The Dakota word for prayer — the ongoing practice of maintaining relationship with all that is sacred — is ____.
    Answer

    wóčhekiye

    wóčhekiye is both a noun (prayer, an act of prayer) and describes the practice of relational prayerfulness. It is broader than the English "prayer," encompassing ongoing relationship with the sacred rather than only formal petition.

    12. Complete the sentence: Wičhášta kiŋ ksapá ____. (The man is wise.)
    Answer

    The sentence Wičhášta kiŋ ksapá is already complete as a predicate construction.

    However, if the exercise asks for the existential verb to make the predicate explicit: waúŋ → Wičhášta kiŋ ksapá waúŋ. — The man exists in a state of wisdom.

    (Note: in Dakota, stative verbs like ksapá can stand alone as predicates without a linking verb; waúŋ makes the construction more explicit.)

    13. The four cardinal Dakota virtues are: _, _, _, and _.
    Answer
    1. wačháŋtognaka (generosity)
    2. ohítika (bravery)
    3. ksapá (wisdom)
    4. čhaŋtéčhila (love / cherishing)

    These four virtues were publicly demonstrated through community action — give-aways, acts of protection, council wisdom, and the raising of children with care.

    Part C — Short Answer (3 questions, 10 points each)¶

    14. Explain the full Dakota meaning of Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ — including its morpheme breakdown — and describe how its ceremonial use expresses a Dakota view of selfhood that differs from Western individualism.
    Rubric / Sample Answer

    Full credit (10 pts): Student provides accurate morpheme breakdown (mi- + tákuye + oyásʼiŋ), correct translation, and a substantive reflection on how the phrase encodes a relational, non-individualistic view of the self — one that claims kinship with all life, not just human community.

    Partial credit (5–7 pts): Correct breakdown and translation but shallow or absent reflection on the philosophical/cultural dimension.

    Sample answer: "Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ breaks down as mi- (my/mine) + tákuye (relations, from takuya = to be related to) + oyásʼiŋ (all, every one). The full meaning is 'all those related to me' or 'all my relations.' The possessive mi- is essential: the speaker is not making a general philosophical statement about the universe — they are declaring personal kinship with all living beings. This challenges Western individualism, which treats the self as a bounded, autonomous unit. The Dakota self, as expressed in Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ, is relationally constituted — you exist because you are in relationship. The phrase closes ceremony as a reminder that every prayer, every action, every word ripples outward into all these relationships."

    15. Describe the role of the four cardinal virtues (wačháŋtognaka, ohítika, ksapá, čhaŋtéčhila) in traditional Dakota communal life. How were these virtues demonstrated publicly, and what does this reveal about the relationship between individual virtue and communal health?
    Rubric / Sample Answer

    Full credit (10 pts): Student accurately describes all four virtues and their communal — not merely individual — expression. Student explains at least one form of public demonstration (give-away, battle protection, council deliberation, child-raising) and reflects on how individual virtue strengthened the tiyóšpaye.

    Partial credit (5–7 pts): Student describes virtues correctly but focuses only on individual dimension without connecting to communal health.

    Sample answer: "The four cardinal virtues were not private moral qualities in Dakota culture — they were communal responsibilities demonstrated through action. Wačháŋtognaka (generosity) was shown through the wóhanpi give-away, where a family distributed goods to the community; the most generous person was most honored. Ohítika (bravery) was not recklessness but deliberate courage in protection of the tiyóšpaye — demonstrated in battle and in speaking difficult truths in council. Ksapá (wisdom) was earned through decades of listening and experience; elders who possessed it were consulted on all major decisions. Čhaŋtéčhila (love/cherishing) was demonstrated through the raising of children and the care of elders. Together, these virtues operated as the connective tissue of community health. A person who was truly generous, brave, wise, and loving strengthened the entire band — and their honored status reflected this communal benefit."

    16. What was the Code of Indian Offenses (1883), what specific Dakota practices did it target, and what forms of resistance did Dakota communities use to maintain their spiritual traditions? Connect your answer to at least two vocabulary items from this chapter.
    Rubric / Sample Answer

    Full credit (10 pts): Student accurately describes the Code of Indian Offenses, names at least two targeted practices, describes at least one form of resistance (continuation in secret, at night, etc.), and connects to at least two vocabulary items from Chapter 5 (e.g., inípi, wiwáŋyag wačhípi, čheyáka, wóčhekiye).

    Partial credit (5–7 pts): Accurate description of the Code but weak connection to vocabulary, or strong vocabulary connection but incomplete description of resistance.

    Sample answer: "The Code of Indian Offenses was a set of federal regulations enacted in 1883 that criminalized many indigenous spiritual and cultural practices. Specifically, it targeted the Sun Dance (wiwáŋyag wačhípi — 'dance looking at the sun'), the inípi (sweat lodge purification ceremony), and the practices of medicine people. Violations could result in imprisonment and the withholding of rations — a devastating punishment for communities already deprived of their land and subsistence base. Yet Dakota communities did not abandon these practices. They continued inípi ceremonies in hidden locations, maintained wóčhekiye (prayer) privately within families, and passed down knowledge of čheyáka (sage) and its ceremonial uses through trusted networks. This resistance was not passive — it was active cultural survival, maintained at personal risk across nearly a century until the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 restored legal protections."
