    Chapter 1 — Háu / Haŋ: Greetings & Identity¶

    Summary¶

    Chapter 1 introduces Dakota greetings, introductions, and identity. Students encounter one of Dakota's most distinctive features immediately: men say Háu to greet, women say Haŋ. Identity in Dakota is rooted in the tiyóšpaye — the extended family band — and in one's relationship to the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ (Seven Council Fires). AIS 112 content covers the pre-contact political structure of the Dakota nation.

    AIS 112 Cultural Context — Weeks 1–4¶

    The Očhéthi Šakówiŋ — Seven Council Fires

    Long before European contact, the Dakota people were organized into the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ — the Seven Council Fires. These were not separate tribes but seven related peoples sharing a common language family and political structure. The Dakota (four eastern fires) lived primarily in Minnesota's lakes and forests; the Nakota (middle fires) lived on the eastern plains; the Lakota (western fire) lived on the northern Great Plains.

    Decision-making was communal and consensus-based. Leadership was earned through demonstrated wisdom, bravery, and generosity — not inherited. The akíčhita (soldier societies) helped maintain community order.

    Tiyóšpaye

    The tiyóšpaye (extended family band) was the basic unit of Dakota society — a group of related families who camped, hunted, and governed together. Identity began with your tiyóšpaye, not with the individual. A Dakota person introduced themselves by naming their tiyóšpaye and their place within it.

    Concepts Covered¶

    1. Male greeting: Háu | Female greeting: Haŋ
    2. Thank you: Pidámayaye (Dakota) / Philámayaye (Lakota)
    3. Yes / No: Háŋ / Hiyá
    4. Goodbye: Tokhéya / Čhaŋtéčhilapi (we love you — closing blessing)
    5. What is your name? Táku eníčiyapi he?
    6. My name is: ___ emáčiyapi
    7. Where are you from? Tókheya yaúŋ he?
    8. I am from / I am: ___ maúŋ
    9. Tiyóšpaye vocabulary and the seven council fires
    10. Personal pronouns: miye (I/me), niye (you), iye (he/she)
    11. The greeting's deeper meaning — relation and acknowledgment
    12. Numbers 1–5 (preview of Chapter 2)

    Prerequisites¶

    No prior Dakota knowledge required. Review Pronunciation Guide — especially the háu/haŋ distinction and accent marks — before beginning.

    Introduction¶

    The first thing Dakota students notice is that greetings have a gender distinction: men say Háu, women say Haŋ. This is not about hierarchy — it is about clarity and tradition in a language that encodes social relationships deeply. Both forms mean essentially the same thing: a warm acknowledgment of the person in front of you.

    The concept closest to Dakota identity is the tiyóšpaye — your extended family group. Before you share your name in traditional Dakota, you share your people. Who you are is inseparable from who raised you, who you belong to, and who you are responsible for.

    Section 1 — Greetings¶

    Core Greeting Vocabulary¶

    Dakota Pronunciation English Notes
    Háu HOW Hello (male) Men's greeting
    Haŋ HON Hello (female) Women's greeting
    Pidámayaye PEE-dah-MAH-yah-yeh Thank you Dakota dialect
    Háŋ HAHN Yes Same for both genders
    Hiyá HEE-yah No
    Tokhéya toh-KHE-yah Goodbye / farewell
    Tókheča šni TOH-kheh-chah SHNEE You're welcome / it's nothing Lit. "it's not different"
    Léčhaŋna LEH-chahn-nah See you soon

    Grammar: Gender Speech in Dakota

    The male/female speech distinction (Háu vs. Haŋ) is one of the most famous features of Dakota and Lakota. It extends beyond greetings to sentence-final particles: males may add -yelo or -lo to statements; females use -ye or softer endings. In this course, both forms are taught so students understand and recognize them in context.

    Morpheme Analysis: Pidámayaye¶

    Word Breakdown: Pidámayaye — "thank you"
    Part Form Meaning
    ROOT pidá- to be pleased / glad
    PREFIX -ma- me (first person object)
    SUFFIX -yaye you cause / you make

    Analysis: "Thank you" in Dakota literally means "you cause me to be glad / you make me pleased." Gratitude is expressed as an action the giver performs on the receiver — a fundamentally relational framing. Compare with Ojibwe miigwech, which is rooted in the concept of giving.

    Example Conversation 1¶

    Dakota English
    A (male): Háu! Hello!
    B (female): Haŋ! Pidámayaye bi-yápi. Hello! Thank you for coming.
    A: Háŋ, pidámayaye. Yes, thank you.
    B: Tokhéya. Goodbye.

    Section 2 — Names & Identity¶

    Introduction Vocabulary¶

    Dakota English Notes
    Táku eníčiyapi he? What is your name? Lit. "What do they call you?"
    ___ emáčiyapi My name is ___ Lit. "They call me ___"
    Tókheya yaúŋ he? Where are you from?
    ___ etáŋhaŋ waúŋ I am from ___
    Mitiyóšpaye My tiyóšpaye / my family band
    Očhéthi Šakówiŋ Seven Council Fires the Dakota nation

    Grammar: Names as Passive Constructions

    Dakota names work differently from English. Instead of "My name IS ," Dakota says "They CALL ME " — emáčiyapi. This passive construction reflects how identity in Dakota is socially conferred, not self-declared. You are named by your community.

    • emáčiyapi = they call me (what my name is)
    • eníčiyapi = they call you (what your name is)
    • ečhíyapi = they call him/her

    Morpheme Analysis: Emáčiyapi¶

    Word Breakdown: emáčiyapi — "they call me / my name is"
    Part Form Meaning
    ROOT e- to say / to call
    INFIX -má- me (first person object)
    INFIX -či- you (second person — in some forms this is absorbed)
    SUFFIX -yapi they (third person plural actor)

    Full meaning: "They [the people] call me [by this name]." Identity is socially bestowed — your name exists because others give it to you and call you by it.

    Personal Pronouns¶

    Dakota English Notes
    Míye I / me independent pronoun
    Níye You
    Iye He / She / It no gender distinction
    Uŋkíyepi We (inclusive) includes the listener
    Uŋkíyepi šni We (exclusive) does not include listener
    Niyépi You all
    Iyépi They

    Dakota English

    Waȟpékhute Wahpekute — "Shooters among the Leaves"
    Sisíthuŋwaŋ Sisseton — "Village of the Swamp"
    Waȟpéthuŋwaŋ Wahpeton — "Village in the Leaves"
    Tiyóšpaye extended family band
    Oyáte nation / people
    Akíčhita soldier / warrior society
    Wičhášta man (wíčhaša in some dialects)
    Wiŋyaŋ woman

    AIS 112 Connection — Pre-Contact Political Structure

    The Dakota operated without a centralized government or hereditary chieftainship in the European sense. Leadership was fluid and earned: a man or woman demonstrated leadership through generosity (wačháŋtognaka), bravery (ohítika), and wisdom (ksapá). The Council of Seven Fires coordinated through annual gatherings and through shared cultural identity rather than through a bureaucratic chain of command.

    This decentralized, consensus-based political structure was systematically misunderstood by U.S. treaty negotiators, who often signed agreements with individual leaders who did not have the authority to cede land on behalf of the entire people.

    Section 3 — Full Introduction¶

    Model Conversation¶

    Dakota English
    A (male): Háu! Táku eníčiyapi he? Hello! What is your name?
    B (female): Haŋ! Sarah emáčiyapi. Gíčhi? Hello! My name is Sarah. And you?
    A: David emáčiyapi. My name is David.
    B: Tókheya yaúŋ he? Where are you from?
    A: Bdewákhaŋthuŋwaŋ etáŋhaŋ waúŋ. I am from Mdewakanton.
    B: Pidámayaye! Tokhéya. Thank you! Goodbye.

    Practice Exercises¶

    Exercise 1 — Greeting Practice

    Practice saying both Háu and Haŋ aloud. Which would you use? Write a short paragraph explaining the gender speech distinction in Dakota and why it matters for a language learner to understand.

    Exercise 2 — Write Your Introduction

    Write a 4-line Dakota introduction including: a greeting, your name using emáčiyapi, where you are from using etáŋhaŋ waúŋ, and a closing Pidámayaye and farewell.

    Exercise 3 — Seven Council Fires Research

    Choose one of the four Dakota council fires (Bdewákhaŋthuŋwaŋ, Waȟpékhute, Sisíthuŋwaŋ, or Waȟpéthuŋwaŋ). Research: Where did they live? What is the meaning of their name? Where is their community located today? Write a 5-sentence summary.
