    Chapter 7 Quiz — Wóoyake: Story, Ceremony & Oral Tradition¶

    Part A — Multiple Choice (10 questions, 2 points each)¶

    1. What does wóoyake mean, and what does its grammatical structure reveal about Dakota storytelling?
    Answer

    b) Story/oral narrative — a nominalized verb meaning "the telling," revealing that story exists only in the act of narration

    wóoyake = wó- (nominalizer) + -oyake (to tell/narrate). Story in Dakota is not a static text but a nominalized action — "the telling." This grammatical structure encodes the oral tradition's understanding that a story only lives in its performance.

    a) Story — a noun referring to a written record of past events b) Story/oral narrative — a nominalized verb meaning "the telling," revealing that story exists only in the act of narration c) Prayer — a sacred chant passed between generations d) Memory — the act of remembering ancestors

    2. Who is Iktómi in Dakota oral tradition?
    Answer

    c) The Spider Trickster — a complex figure who teaches through failure and self-deception

    Iktómi (spider/trickster) is simultaneously foolish and clever, dangerous and creative. His stories are told with laughter because the audience is meant to see themselves in him. He breaks rules and thereby reveals why the rules exist.

    a) A great warrior who defeated the U.S. Army b) A sacred spider who created the first humans c) The Spider Trickster — a complex figure who teaches through failure and self-deception d) A benevolent elder spirit who brings good harvests

    3. What is the function of the discourse marker eyápi in Dakota storytelling?
    Answer

    b) An evidential marker meaning "they say / it is said" — signaling that the knowledge comes from tradition, not personal witness

    eyápi (from eyá = to say, -pi = plural) signals that the speaker received this knowledge from others — from tradition, elders, or oral transmission. This is an important epistemic marker that acknowledges the chain of transmission.

    a) A closing marker that ends the story b) An evidential marker meaning "they say / it is said" — signaling that the knowledge comes from tradition, not personal witness c) A time marker meaning "long ago" d) A contrast marker meaning "but"

    4. Which discourse marker opens a narrative and can also close it in Dakota storytelling?
    Answer

    a) oháŋ

    oháŋ functions both as a story-opening marker ("once / it is said") and as a story-closing affirmation ("so it is / such is the story"). This dual use mirrors the circular structure of many Dakota narratives.

    a) oháŋ b) héčhel c) heháŋ d) eháŋni

    5. According to the winter telling protocols, why may certain stories only be told during winter months?
    Answer

    c) The creatures who might be disturbed or angered by hearing certain stories are dormant in winter

    The winter telling protocol is not arbitrary. It reflects an ecological and spiritual logic: specific stories involving Iktómi and spirit beings cannot be safely told when insects, snakes, and other creatures are awake and might overhear. This protocol survived the boarding school era in many families.

    a) Winter nights are longer, giving more time for storytelling b) The stories are about winter events and are contextually appropriate c) The creatures who might be disturbed or angered by hearing certain stories are dormant in winter d) Elders are most available in winter when there is less subsistence work

    6. In the Iktómi story fragment, what grammatical role does tká play in the sentence Yá kte tká hí šni?
    Answer

    b) Contrast marker — "but"

    tká marks a contrast between what was expected or intended and what actually happened. Yá kte tká hí šni = "He was going to go but he didn't arrive/come." This is the contrast discourse marker, equivalent to English "but" or "however."

    a) Sequential marker — "and then" b) Contrast marker — "but" c) Evidential marker — "they say" d) Opening marker — "once"

    7. What did the boarding school system do to Dakota oral tradition?
    Answer

    b) By separating children from grandparents (the primary story-keepers) and forbidding Dakota, it broke the chain of transmission, permanently losing many stories

    Grandparents were the primary keepers of story in Dakota culture. The boarding school system physically removed children from this relationship and forbidden them to speak Dakota — making it impossible to receive stories that required specific time, permission, and language.

    a) It documented oral tradition in written form, preserving it b) By separating children from grandparents (the primary story-keepers) and forbidding Dakota, it broke the chain of transmission, permanently losing many stories c) It allowed oral tradition to continue in a modified English form d) It had little impact because stories were also preserved in pictographic form

    8. The discourse marker héčhel signals:
    Answer

    c) A consequence — "and so / so it happened"

    héčhel marks a logical or narrative consequence: because X, héčhel Y. "And so he went." "And so it happened." It is different from heháŋ (simple sequential "then") in that it implies cause-effect or narrative momentum.

    a) Opening of the story b) Simple sequence — "and then" c) A consequence — "and so / so it happened" d) Reported speech — "they say"

    9. The word oháŋka means:
    Answer

    b) To tell a story / to narrate

    oháŋka is the verb "to tell, to narrate." The noun form wóoyake is derived from this root with the nominalizing prefix wó-. oháŋka and wóoyake are related as "to tell" is related to "the telling."

    a) To remember b) To tell a story / to narrate c) To pray d) To gather for ceremony

    10. Why is the revitalization of oral tradition described as an act of 'knowledge sovereignty' in the AIS 112 content?
    Answer

    b) Because oral tradition carries a complete epistemology — a way of knowing the world — that does not require external validation, and its revitalization asserts the validity and completeness of Dakota knowledge systems

    Knowledge sovereignty means asserting that one's own knowledge systems are valid and complete without needing Western institutional validation. Dakota oral tradition is not merely cultural heritage — it is a full epistemology: it encodes ecology, ethics, cosmology, and relational knowledge.

    a) Because oral tradition was protected under the Treaty of Traverse des Sioux b) Because oral tradition carries a complete epistemology — a way of knowing the world — that does not require external validation, and its revitalization asserts the validity and completeness of Dakota knowledge systems c) Because the U.S. government officially acknowledged oral tradition as a legal form of history d) Because storytelling generates revenue for tribal language programs

    Part B — Fill in the Blank (3 questions, 5 points each)¶

    11. Complete this story opening in Dakota: ____ Iktómi tȟáŋka eyápi. (Long ago, Iktómi was great, they say.)
    Answer

    Eháŋni

    Eháŋni Iktómi tȟáŋka eyápi. — eháŋni = long ago / in the old days. It is the narrative temporal opener, equivalent to "once upon a time." Note also eyápi at the end, the evidential "they say."

    12. The discourse marker that signals simple narrative sequence — 'and then' — is ____.
    Answer

    čha (also acceptable: heháŋ)

    čha is the simple connective "and then" linking two sequential events: Yúta čha yá = "He ate and then went." heháŋ is slightly stronger, marking a more explicit temporal sequence: "Then, at that point..."

    13. In Dakota oral tradition, the chain of story transmission was most directly broken when ____ were separated from their grandparents in boarding schools.
    Answer

    Children (Dakota children / Dakota youth)

    Grandparents were the primary keepers of story in Dakota culture. The physical removal of children to distant boarding schools, combined with prohibition on speaking Dakota, severed this intergenerational transmission. Many stories that required elder knowledge, family permission, or winter-telling conditions could not survive this separation.

    Part C — Short Answer (3 questions, 10 points each)¶

    14. Analyze the Iktómi story fragment from Chapter 7. What does Iktómi do wrong, what are the consequences, and what does this reveal about how Dakota oral tradition uses the trickster figure as a teaching tool?
    Rubric / Sample Answer

    Full credit (10 pts): Student identifies Iktómi's boast (claiming to have a fast horse when he has none), the consequence (the dog takes the [imaginary] horse, Iktómi is fooled), and reflects substantively on what the trickster figure achieves — teaching through failure, using humor, and inviting self-recognition rather than moralizing.

    Partial credit (5–7 pts): Student identifies the action and consequence but does not reflect on the teaching function of the trickster.

    Sample answer: "In the Iktómi fragment, Iktómi boasts to a dog that his horse is fast — but he has no horse (tká čhaŋpá yuká šni = but he had no horse). He then falls asleep, and the dog takes the (nonexistent) horse. Iktómi is made a fool. The lesson is not delivered through a moral at the end — there is no narrator saying 'and so children, do not lie.' Instead, the story simply ends: Iktómi was fooled. The audience is left to laugh and to recognize themselves. This is the trickster's teaching method: not instruction but mirror. Iktómi's boast is recognizably human — we all inflate ourselves. His failure is also recognizably human. The story works because the audience laughs at Iktómi and, in doing so, laughs at themselves without shame."

    15. Explain the function of narrative discourse markers in Dakota storytelling. How do eháŋni, héčhel, heháŋ, čha, and tká work together to structure a narrative, and how do they differ from English narrative conventions?
    Rubric / Sample Answer

    Full credit (10 pts): Student accurately explains all five markers with their specific functions (opening, consequence, sequential, simple connective, contrast) and reflects on how Dakota discourse structure differs from English (no past-tense verb forms for narrative past; context and markers carry time information).

    Partial credit (5–7 pts): Student explains three or more markers correctly but does not compare to English conventions.

    Sample answer: "Dakota storytelling uses a set of discourse markers to structure narrative time and logic without relying on verb tense. Eháŋni opens the story in the past ('long ago'); héčhel marks a consequence ('and so it happened'); heháŋ marks simple temporal sequence ('then'); čha is a simple connective ('and then'); and tká marks contrast ('but'). Together, they give a narrative its shape — opening, sequence, surprise, and contrast — using particles rather than verb inflections. In English, narrative past is carried largely by past-tense verb forms ('he went,' 'she said'). In Dakota, the verb forms do not change for narrative past; the markers do the work. This means a Dakota listener attends to discourse-level structure — the markers — to understand narrative time, rather than word-level verb morphology."

    16. What does it mean for oral tradition to function as 'knowledge sovereignty,' and why is the revitalization of Dakota storytelling inseparable from language revitalization? Use at least two specific examples from the chapter.
    Rubric / Sample Answer

    Full credit (10 pts): Student accurately explains knowledge sovereignty (assertion that indigenous knowledge systems are complete and valid without external validation), explains why language is the carrier of the epistemology (specific vocabulary, evidential markers, discourse structure), and gives at least two specific examples from Chapter 7 (e.g., the evidential eyápi, the winter-telling protocols, the Iktómi stories' teaching method).

    Partial credit (5–7 pts): Good explanation of knowledge sovereignty but weak connection to specific chapter examples.

    Sample answer: "Knowledge sovereignty means asserting that a community's knowledge systems are internally complete and do not require validation from outside institutions to be legitimate. For Dakota communities, oral tradition is not 'folk history' waiting to be validated by academic historians — it is a full epistemology encoding ecology (čhaŋwápe-kasná-wi = leaf-falling moon, telling you what time of year to prepare for winter), ethics (Iktómi stories teaching through failure and self-recognition), and relational knowledge (the evidential eyápi acknowledging the chain of transmission). This knowledge system lives in the language. The evidential marker eyápi — 'they say' — is not translatable as a simple word; it encodes an entire epistemological stance about how knowledge is received and transmitted. Without the Dakota language, eyápi becomes meaningless. The winter-telling protocols, similarly, cannot be adequately expressed in English — the logic of who can hear which stories, and when, is embedded in a worldview that requires the language to carry it. Revitalizing the language is therefore revitalizing the knowledge sovereignty itself."
