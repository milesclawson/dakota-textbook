    Chapter 2 — Otóiyohi: Numbers, Colors & Descriptors¶

    Summary¶

    Chapter 2 introduces the Dakota number system from one to ten, a set of color terms that function as stative verbs rather than adjectives, and descriptive vocabulary for everyday objects. Students learn one of Dakota's most fundamental grammatical distinctions: in Dakota, words like "red" or "white" are not adjectives modifying a noun — they are stative verbs describing a state of being. AIS 112 content introduces the Oceti Sakowin's relationship with the land and how colors held sacred significance in Dakota ceremony.

    AIS 112 Cultural Context — Weeks 3–5¶

    Colors in Dakota Ceremony and Worldview

    In Dakota spiritual practice, the four cardinal directions are associated with specific colors that carry meaning far beyond aesthetics. Red (šá) is associated with the east and new beginnings. Black (sápa) is associated with the west and transformation. White (sáŋ) is associated with the north and purity. Yellow-gold (žiží) is associated with the south and growth. These associations appear in ceremony, regalia, and in the decoration of sacred objects. When you learn Dakota color words, you are also learning a layer of cosmological meaning.

    Dakota Counting and Trade

    Before European contact, the Dakota peoples used a decimal counting system that enabled trade, measurement of time, and the counting of horses, bison, and winter counts. The waniyetu wowapi (winter count) was a pictographic calendar kept on bison hide; keepers of these counts tracked years by significant events. Numbers were essential to this practice. The Dakota decimal system — base 10 — mirrors the structure of many world languages and suggests a long tradition of quantitative thinking.

    Stative Verbs vs. Adjectives

    A common error for English speakers is to treat Dakota color words as adjectives that come before a noun ("the red horse"). In Dakota, color words are stative verbs — they describe a condition or state. The correct structure places the color word after the noun: šúŋka kiŋ šá = "the dog, it-is-red" (the red dog). This verb-like behavior is consistent with how Dakota grammar treats most descriptive states.

    Concepts Covered¶

    1. Numbers 1–10: waŋží, núŋpa, yámni, tópa, záptaŋ, šákpe, šakówiŋ, šaglóğaŋ, napčíyuŋka, wikčémna
    2. Numbers 11–20: compound formation with wikčémna
    3. Colors as stative verbs: šá, tó, ğí, sáŋ, sápa, žiží
    4. Color-noun construction: noun + kiŋ + color-verb
    5. Stative verb conjugation: first and second person forms
    6. Counting objects: tóna (how many?)
    7. Basic descriptor vocabulary: čísčila (small), tháŋka (big), téča (new), kȟáŋta (old)
    8. The suffix -la / -na as a diminutive/softener
    9. Sacred and ceremonial color associations
    10. Winter count (waniyetu wowapi) and Dakota record-keeping
    11. Number compounds: wikčémna núŋpa (twenty), wikčémna yámni (thirty)
    12. Asking and stating quantities in context

    Prerequisites¶

    Chapter 1 — Háu / Haŋ: Greetings & Identity — Complete before beginning Chapter 2. Students should be comfortable with Dakota pronunciation, accent marks, and the concept of gendered greetings.

    Introduction¶

    When English speakers learn a new language, they often expect to find adjectives that work just like English ones — words placed before a noun that describe it. Dakota surprises students here. There are no adjectives in the traditional English sense. Instead, Dakota uses stative verbs — verb forms that describe a state of being — to express what English calls descriptive qualities. "Red" is not a property a horse has; it is a state the horse is in.

    This grammatical distinction is not a quirk. It reflects something deeper about how Dakota organizes the world: states, conditions, and qualities are dynamic, not fixed. A thing can be red the way a fire can be burning. This philosophy of ongoing relationship between objects and their qualities is woven through the language.

    Numbers in Dakota follow a similar logic of relationship and combination. Once you know the numbers one through ten, you can construct all numbers up to one hundred through a transparent, logical system of combination. The word for eleven is literally "ten and one more." The elegance of this system made it practical for trade, ceremony, and the keeping of records across generations.

    Section 1 — Numbers 1–20¶

    Key Vocabulary: Cardinal Numbers¶

    Dakota Pronunciation English Notes
    waŋží WAHN-zhee one Also means "a/an" (indefinite article)
    núŋpa NOON-pah two
    yámni YAH-mnee three
    tópa TOH-pah four
    záptaŋ ZAP-tahn five
    šákpe SHAHK-peh six
    šakówiŋ shah-KOH-wihn seven
    šaglóğaŋ shah-GLOH-gahn eight
    napčíyuŋka nap-CHEE-yuhn-kah nine Lit. "one bent back" (nine fingers)
    wikčémna wik-CHEHM-nah ten

    Morpheme Analysis: napčíyuŋka¶

    Break down the word for "nine"
    Morpheme Meaning
    napč- from nápe (hand)
    -í- directional/reflexive infix
    -yuŋka to lie/bend back

    Analysis: The word for nine encodes a body-based counting gesture — nine fingers with one "bent back" or held down. This is a trace of a tactile, embodied counting system where the body itself was the original counting tool. Many indigenous number systems show similar somatic origins. When students learn napčíyuŋka, they are preserving memory of how Dakota people first counted — with their hands.

    Numbers 11–20: Compound Formation¶

    Dakota English Structure
    wikčémna waŋží aké waŋží eleven ten + one + more/again + one
    wikčémna waŋží aké núŋpa twelve ten + one + more + two
    wikčémna waŋží aké yámni thirteen ten + one + more + three
    wikčémna waŋží aké tópa fourteen ten + one + more + four
    wikčémna waŋží aké záptaŋ fifteen ten + one + more + five
    wikčémna núŋpa twenty ten × two
    wikčémna yámni thirty ten × three

    Grammar: The Compound Number Pattern

    Numbers above ten are formed transparently: wikčémna (ten) + the tens multiplier + aké (again/more) + the ones digit. This additive, transparent structure means you never need to memorize arbitrary forms — you build them from parts you already know.

    Example Sentences: Counting¶

    Dakota English
    Šúŋka núŋpa yuhápi. They have two dogs.
    Tóna yukhéha he? How many do you have?
    Waŋží héčhiŋyaŋ. Just one.
    Wikčémna waŋží čhaŋpá yuhápi. They have ten horses.
    Záptaŋ wičhášta kiŋ hí. Five men came.

    Section 2 — Colors as Stative Verbs¶

    Grammar: Stative Verbs for Color

    In Dakota, color words function as intransitive stative verbs. This means they conjugate for person and can appear as predicates. The basic construction is:

    [Noun] + kiŋ + [color-verb]

    • Šúŋka kiŋ šá. = The dog is red.
    • Wówapi kiŋ tó. = The book is blue/green.

    To make a color word modify a noun in an English adjective-like way, Dakota uses a relative clause structure or a nominalizer suffix.

    Key Vocabulary: Colors¶

    Dakota Pronunciation English Notes
    šá SHAH red Also: crimson, scarlet; sacred in ceremony
    tó TOH blue / green One word covers both blue and green
    ğí GHEE brown / tan / yellow Earth tones; also describes tawny animals
    sáŋ SAHN white / whitish Off-white, cream; winter associations
    sápa SAH-pah black Night, the west, transformation
    žiží ZHEE-zhee yellow-orange Golden; sun, south, growth
    lúta LOO-tah bright red More intense than šá; sometimes ceremonial

    Morpheme Analysis: sápa¶

    Break down the word for "black"
    Morpheme Meaning
    sá- a root suggesting brightness/contrast
    -pa a suffix indicating completeness or depth

    Analysis: The word sápa for black is interesting because it shares a root with sáŋ (white/whitish) — both seem to involve a base related to perceptual contrast. This root pattern suggests that the Dakota color system may have originally organized colors around degrees of light/darkness before expanding into hue distinctions. The pairing of sáŋ and sápa mirrors the pairing of north and west in ceremonial direction symbolism.

    Color Conjugation: First and Second Person¶

    Stative verbs conjugate by inserting a personal prefix. For color verbs, the first-person prefix is ma- and the second-person prefix is ni-:

    Person Dakota English
    I am (red) mašá I am red (describing my state/blush)
    You are (red) ništá You are red
    He/She/It is (red) šá (it) is red — no prefix
    I am (black) masápa I am black (I am wearing black/in darkness)
    You are (black) nisápa You are black

    Example Sentences: Colors¶

    Dakota English
    Čhaŋpá kiŋ žiží. The horse is golden/yellow.
    Maȟpíya kiŋ tó. The sky is blue.
    Makȟá kiŋ ğí. The earth is brown.
    Wápa kiŋ sáŋ. The snow is white.
    Háŋpa kiŋ sápa. The moccasin is black.
    Čhaŋpá šá čhiŋ hí. The red horse came.

    Section 3 — Descriptors and Size Words¶

    Key Vocabulary: Basic Descriptors¶

    Dakota Pronunciation English Notes
    čísčila CHEES-chee-lah small, little Diminutive -la adds softness
    tháŋka THAHN-kah big, large
    téča TEH-chah new
    kȟáŋta KHAHN-tah old (of objects) Do not use for people
    waŋká WAHN-kah old (of people) Respectful; use only for elders
    čhaŋtéčhila chahn-TEH-chee-lah beloved, dear Lit. "heart-small" = treasured
    téča šni TEH-chah SHNEE not new, old Negation with šni
    tȟáŋka šni THAHN-kah SHNEE not big, small

    AIS 112 Connection: Diminutives and Kinship

    The diminutive suffix -la / -na appears throughout Dakota in terms of endearment and kinship. Čhuwítkala (younger sister, female speaking) contains -kala as a diminutive of čhuwítka. This suffix signals smallness but also tenderness — the small one who is beloved. Understanding diminutives is essential for reading Dakota kinship terms in Chapter 4.

    Example Sentences: Descriptors¶

    Dakota English
    Tipi kiŋ tháŋka. The house/lodge is big.
    Šúŋka kiŋ čísčila. The dog is small.
    Wówapi kiŋ téča. The book is new.
    Wičhášta kiŋ waŋká. The man is old (elder).
    Čísčila tȟáŋka šni. The small one is not big.

    Practice Exercises¶

    Exercise 1: Counting Around You

    Look up from this page and count five objects you can see. Say their count in Dakota: - Waŋží... (One...) - Núŋpa... (Two...) - Yámni... (Three...) - Tópa... (Four...) - Záptaŋ... (Five...)

    Now answer aloud: Tóna he? (How many?) — Záptaŋ. (Five.)

    Exercise 2: Color the World

    For each item below, compose a Dakota sentence using the stative verb pattern: [Noun] + kiŋ + [color-verb]

    1. The sky (maȟpíya) — blue
    2. The tree (čháŋ) — brown
    3. Snow (wápa) — white
    4. Your shirt (use oğánke) — choose a color

    Sample answer for #1: Maȟpíya kiŋ tó.

    Exercise 3: Describe and Identify

    Translate these phrases into Dakota: 1. The small red dog 2. Two big horses 3. Ten new books 4. One old woman (use wiŋyaŋ waŋká)

    Hints: Use [noun] + [number] for items 2–4, and remember that the color follows the noun as a stative verb clause.
