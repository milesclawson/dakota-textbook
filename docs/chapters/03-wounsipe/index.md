# Chapter 3 — Wóuŋsipi: Daily Life & Action Verbs

## Summary

Chapter 3 introduces the Dakota action verb system, daily-life vocabulary, and the language's fundamental SOV (Subject-Object-Verb) word order. Students learn how to conjugate active verbs in the present and future tenses, using the future suffix **-kte** and the negation suffix **-šni**. AIS 112 content covers the structure of traditional Dakota daily life — the rhythms of the tiyóšpaye across seasons — and how language reflected communal responsibility over individual action.

## AIS 112 Cultural Context — Weeks 5–7

!!! info "Daily Life in the Tiyóšpaye"
    Traditional Dakota daily life was organized around the tiyóšpaye — the extended family band. Responsibilities were distributed by age, gender, and clan role, not by individual preference. Women managed the tipi, processed food, and maintained the camp; men hunted, engaged in diplomacy, and protected the community. Children learned by doing, watching elders, and participating from an early age. There was no sharp separation between "work" and "life" — tending horses, preparing food, telling stories, and praying were all part of a continuous daily practice.

!!! info "Language and Collective Action"
    Dakota verbs encode collective action grammatically. The inclusive first-person plural (*uŋ-...-pi*, "we including you") and exclusive (*uŋ-...-pi* vs. other forms) distinguish whether the listener is part of the group acting. This is not a minor detail — it is a grammatical expression of the Dakota value of **wóčhekiye** (prayer/relatedness): when you say "we," Dakota grammar asks you to specify whether you mean "we with you" or "we without you." English collapses this distinction; Dakota preserves it.

!!! warning "SOV Word Order"
    English word order is SVO: **S**ubject → **V**erb → **O**bject ("The woman eats the berry"). Dakota word order is SOV: **S**ubject → **O**bject → **V**erb ("The woman the-berry eats"). This is one of the most important structural differences to internalize. Do not translate word-by-word from English — restructure the sentence entirely.

## Introduction

Every language has a "skeleton" — a basic sentence structure that everything else hangs from. In English, that skeleton is Subject-Verb-Object: *The hunter sees the deer.* In Dakota, the skeleton is Subject-Object-Verb: *The hunter the-deer sees.* This single difference restructures everything about how you build a sentence. The verb — the action, the heart of what's happening — comes at the end, like a conclusion.

This is not arbitrary. Linguists have observed that SOV languages tend to cluster with certain other features: postpositions rather than prepositions, relative clauses before nouns rather than after them, adjectives (or stative verbs) after nouns rather than before. Dakota is consistent in this way: the language is built so that context accumulates and the core meaning lands last.

Learning to think in SOV order requires unlearning English habits. The exercises in this chapter ask you to build sentences from the ground up, placing the verb last every time — not as a rule to memorize, but as a way of thinking.

### Morpheme Analysis: wayáwa

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Break down the verb "to read / to count"</summary>

| Morpheme | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| wa- | general object prefix (indefinite direct object) |
| -yáwa | to count, to reckon |

**Analysis:** *wayáwa* originally meant "to count" — to reckon numbers. With the arrival of written language and formal schooling, the word expanded to cover reading as well, since reading involves the same act of reckoning symbols. This semantic expansion tells a history: it marks the moment Dakota communities encountered written text as a counting-like cognitive act. The word *wayáwa* also appears in the phrase *wayáwa tí* (school, literally "reading/counting house"), showing how compound nouns preserve historical moments.

</details>

## Section 2 — Verb Conjugation

!!! note "Grammar: Active Verb Conjugation"
    Dakota active verbs mark the subject with infixed or prefixed personal markers. For most active verbs, the pattern is:

    | Person | Prefix/Infix | Example (*yúta* = to eat) |
    |--------|--------------|--------------------------|
    | I (1sg) | wa- | **wa**yúta — I eat |
    | You (2sg) | ya- | **ya**yúta — you eat |
    | He/She (3sg) | (none) | yúta — he/she eats |
    | We (1pl incl.) | uŋ-...-pi | **uŋ**yúta**pi** — we (all) eat |
    | You all (2pl) | ya-...-pi | **ya**yúta**pi** — you all eat |
    | They (3pl) | -pi | yúta**pi** — they eat |

    Note: The *-pi* suffix marks plural for all persons. The verb root stays the same; prefixes and suffixes do the work.

|--------|--------|---------|

## Section 3 — Future Tense and Negation

!!! note "Grammar: Future Tense with -kte"
    The suffix **-kte** attaches to the end of a verb (after any other suffixes) to express future intention or prediction:

    - *Wayúta.* → I eat. (present)
    - *Wayúta kte.* → I will eat. (future)
    - *Yápi kte.* → They will go. (future plural)

    **-kte** is not an auxiliary verb — it is a suffix that bonds to the verb. It can follow the plural *-pi* suffix: *yápi kte* (they will go).

!!! note "Grammar: Negation with -šni"
    The suffix **-šni** attaches to negate a verb:

    - *Wayúta šni.* → I do not eat. / I am not eating.
    - *Wayúta kte šni.* → I will not eat. (future negation: -kte comes before -šni)
    - *Yápi kte šni.* → They will not go.

    The order is always: **verb root + -kte (if future) + -šni (negation)**

### Future and Negation: Full Paradigm for yúta

| Tense | Person | Dakota | English |
|-------|--------|--------|---------|
| Present | I | wayúta | I eat |
| Present neg. | I | wayúta šni | I do not eat |
| Future | I | wayúta kte | I will eat |
| Future neg. | I | wayúta kte šni | I will not eat |
| Future | They | yútapi kte | They will eat |
| Future neg. | They | yútapi kte šni | They will not eat |

### Example Sentences: SOV + Future + Negation

| Dakota | English |
|--------|---------|
| Waúŋ kte. | I will be (here). |
| Wayúta kte šni. | I will not eat. |
| Wičhášta kiŋ wayáwa kte. | The man will read. |
| Wiŋyaŋ kiŋ hí. | The woman came. |
| Šúŋka kiŋ waŋúŋ šni. | The dog is not here (doesn't exist here). |
| Čhiŋkší kiŋ wayáwa kte šni he? | Will the son not read? |

| tȟatéyaŋ | thah-TEH-yahn | wind | |

!!! info "AIS 112 Connection: The Tipi as Living Architecture"
    The word *tipi* literally means "dwelling" (*ti* = to dwell, *pi* = plural marker making it a noun of habitation). The conical buffalo-hide tipi was not a primitive shelter but a sophisticated, portable, climate-responsive dwelling. It could be erected in under an hour, packed onto a travois, and moved with the seasonal migration of the bison herds. The tipi's circular interior had specific zones: the area opposite the entrance was honored space for elders and guests; the fire at center was both heat source and sacred focus. When *tipi* entered English as "tepee," it lost all of this architectural and social meaning. In Dakota, the word still carries it.

| Dakota | English |
|--------|---------|

| Wóyute kiŋ wayúta kte. | I will eat the food. |
| Haŋhépi kiŋ išníla waúŋ kte. | I will be quiet at night. |
| Wayáwa tí kiŋ blé kte. | I will go to school. |
| Wičhášta kiŋ čháŋ kiŋ yuhápi. | The men have the wood. |

## Practice Exercises

!!! example "Exercise 1: Reorder into SOV"
    Rewrite these English-order sentences into correct Dakota SOV order (Dakota words provided, just reorder):

    1. English: "The woman eats the berry." Words: *wičhíŋčala* (woman), *čhaŋpá šápa* (chokecherry), *yúta* (eats)
    2. English: "The child will go to school." Words: *čhiŋkší* (child), *wayáwa tí* (school), *blé kte* (will go)
    3. English: "They do not read." Words: *wayáwapi šni* (they do not read)

    Remember: Subject → Object → Verb.

!!! example "Exercise 2: Conjugate the Verb"
    Conjugate the verb *hí* (to come/arrive) for all six persons (I, you, he/she, we, you all, they). Then add *-kte* for future tense to each form.

    Hint: *hí* is somewhat irregular — *wahí* (I come), *yahí* (you come), *hí* (he/she comes), *uŋhípi* (we come), *yahípi* (you all come), *hípi* (they come).

!!! example "Exercise 3: Build Complete Sentences"
    Using the vocabulary from this chapter, build five complete Dakota sentences — at least two in future tense and one negative. Include subjects, objects, and verbs in SOV order. Write each Dakota sentence with its English translation.

    Example: *Wiŋyaŋ kiŋ wóyute kiŋ wayúta kte šni.* — The woman will not eat the food.
