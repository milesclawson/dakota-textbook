## Part B — Fill in the Blank (3 questions, 5 points each)

??? question "11. Complete this story opening in Dakota: __________ Iktómi tȟáŋka eyápi. (Long ago, Iktómi was great, they say.)"
    ??? answer "Answer"
        **Eháŋni**

        *Eháŋni Iktómi tȟáŋka eyápi.* — *eháŋni* = long ago / in the old days. It is the narrative temporal opener, equivalent to "once upon a time." Note also *eyápi* at the end, the evidential "they say."

??? question "12. The discourse marker that signals simple narrative sequence — 'and then' — is __________."
    ??? answer "Answer"
        **čha** (also acceptable: **heháŋ**)

        *čha* is the simple connective "and then" linking two sequential events: *Yúta čha yá* = "He ate and then went." *heháŋ* is slightly stronger, marking a more explicit temporal sequence: "Then, at that point..."

??? question "13. In Dakota oral tradition, the chain of story transmission was most directly broken when __________ were separated from their grandparents in boarding schools."
    ??? answer "Answer"
        **Children** (Dakota children / Dakota youth)

        Grandparents were the primary keepers of story in Dakota culture. The physical removal of children to distant boarding schools, combined with prohibition on speaking Dakota, severed this intergenerational transmission. Many stories that required elder knowledge, family permission, or winter-telling conditions could not survive this separation.

??? question "15. Explain the function of narrative discourse markers in Dakota storytelling. How do *eháŋni*, *héčhel*, *heháŋ*, *čha*, and *tká* work together to structure a narrative, and how do they differ from English narrative conventions?"
    ??? answer "Rubric / Sample Answer"
        **Full credit (10 pts):** Student accurately explains all five markers with their specific functions (opening, consequence, sequential, simple connective, contrast) and reflects on how Dakota discourse structure differs from English (no past-tense verb forms for narrative past; context and markers carry time information).

        **Partial credit (5–7 pts):** Student explains three or more markers correctly but does not compare to English conventions.

        **Sample answer:** "Dakota storytelling uses a set of discourse markers to structure narrative time and logic without relying on verb tense. *Eháŋni* opens the story in the past ('long ago'); *héčhel* marks a consequence ('and so it happened'); *heháŋ* marks simple temporal sequence ('then'); *čha* is a simple connective ('and then'); and *tká* marks contrast ('but'). Together, they give a narrative its shape — opening, sequence, surprise, and contrast — using particles rather than verb inflections. In English, narrative past is carried largely by past-tense verb forms ('he went,' 'she said'). In Dakota, the verb forms do not change for narrative past; the markers do the work. This means a Dakota listener attends to discourse-level structure — the markers — to understand narrative time, rather than word-level verb morphology."
