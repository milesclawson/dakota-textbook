# Chapter 7 — Wóoyake: Story, Ceremony & Oral Tradition

## Summary

Chapter 7 introduces the vocabulary and grammar of Dakota storytelling, oral tradition, and ceremony. Students learn about *Iktómi* — the trickster spider figure — and practice the narrative verb forms and discourse markers used in oral storytelling. AIS 112 content covers the role of oral tradition in Dakota knowledge preservation, the suppression of storytelling in boarding schools, and the ongoing work of language and story revitalization.

## AIS 112 Cultural Context — Weeks 13–15

!!! info "Wóoyake — The Living Story"
    *Wóoyake* means "story" or "oral narrative," but in Dakota tradition, a story is never merely entertainment. Every traditional story (*wóoyake*) carries teaching (*wóuŋsipi*), spiritual content, and community memory. Stories are not read from books — they are spoken, with specific protocols: certain stories may only be told in winter (when snakes and insects are underground and cannot hear), certain stories belong to specific families or keepers, and storytelling requires the audience to participate actively through verbal acknowledgment.

    The trickster *Iktómi* (Spider) is one of the most important figures in Dakota oral tradition. Unlike simple morality tales, Iktómi stories are complex: the trickster is simultaneously foolish and wise, dangerous and creative. Iktómi breaks rules and in doing so reveals what the rules are for. He is the figure who teaches through failure — his own and ours.

!!! warning "Boarding Schools and the Suppression of Story"
    One of the most systematic cultural losses in the boarding school era was the suppression of oral tradition. When children were separated from their grandparents — the primary keepers of story in Dakota culture — the chain of transmission was broken. Stories that required winter telling, specific family permission, or elder knowledge simply could not be transmitted in an institutional environment where children were forbidden to speak Dakota. Many stories were lost permanently. The revitalization of oral tradition is therefore inseparable from language revitalization — you cannot tell the stories without the language.

!!! info "Winter Telling Protocols"
    In traditional Dakota culture, certain stories — especially those involving *Iktómi* and other spirit beings — may only be told during the winter months, after the first frost and before the last. This protocol is not merely ceremonial. It reflects an ecological and spiritual logic: the creatures who might be disturbed or angered by hearing certain stories are dormant in winter. These protocols survived the boarding school era in many families and communities and are being actively maintained today.

## Introduction

The Dakota word *wóoyake* — story — shares a root with *oháŋka* (to tell, to narrate). In Dakota, telling a story is an action, not a transmission. The teller does not simply relay information; they perform a living relationship between the community, the ancestors, and the world as it exists right now. This is why stories required specific times, specific permissions, and specific audiences.

*Iktómi* — the Spider Trickster — is one of Dakota oral tradition's most enduring teachers. He is brilliant and stupid. He outwits others and then outwits himself. He breaks every rule and in doing so demonstrates, vividly, why the rules exist. A story about Iktómi is never a simple lesson with a moral at the end; it is an invitation to sit with complexity — to see yourself in the foolish, cunning spider and laugh at yourself without shame.

The grammar of storytelling in Dakota has its own patterns. Discourse markers like *héčhel* (and so / then it happened) and *čha* (and then) do the work that "once upon a time" and "and they lived happily ever after" do in English — they signal that a narrative is in progress and mark its transitions. Learning to recognize and use these markers is learning to think in story.

### Morpheme Analysis: wóoyake

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Break down the word for "story"</summary>

| Morpheme | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| wó- | nominalizing prefix (creates a noun from a verb) |
| -oyake | to tell/narrate (related to *oháŋka*) |

**Analysis:** *wóoyake* is a nominalization of the verb "to tell" — the story is literally "the telling." In Dakota, story is not a static object (a text, a document) but an action nominalized — frozen in noun form but carrying the energy of telling within it. This grammatical structure encodes the oral tradition's understanding: a story does not exist apart from the telling. Every telling is a new story, shaped by its teller, its audience, and its time.

</details>

### Morpheme Analysis: Iktómi

<details markdown="1">
<summary>The name and nature of the Spider Trickster</summary>

| Element | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| Iktómi | Spider; the trickster figure |

**Analysis:** The name *Iktómi* comes from the word for spider (*iktómi* = spider in some dialects). The spider is an apt trickster: it creates elaborate webs (deceits, schemes, traps) and sometimes catches itself. Iktómi in Dakota oral tradition is not a devil or a pure evil — he is a complex figure who models human behavior at its worst and most creative simultaneously. His stories are told with laughter. The audience is meant to recognize themselves. This is story as mirror, not as lesson.

</details>

## Section 2 — Narrative Grammar

!!! note "Grammar: Discourse Markers in Storytelling"
    Dakota narrative uses specific discourse markers to signal story structure. Unlike English, Dakota does not use tense suffixes to mark "past" in narrative — the discourse markers and context do this work:

    | Marker | Use | Example |
    |--------|-----|---------|
    | *oháŋ* or *eháŋni* | Opens the story ("Once...") | *Eháŋni Iktómi...* = Long ago, Iktómi... |
    | *héčhel* | Signals a consequence ("And so...") | *Héčhel yá.* = And so he went. |
    | *heháŋ* | Sequential ("Then...") | *Heháŋ wičhášta kiŋ hí.* = Then the man came. |
    | *čha* | Simple sequence ("And then...") | *Yúta čha yá.* = He ate and then went. |
    | *tká* | Contrast ("But...") | *Yá kte tká hí šni.* = He was going to go but he didn't come. |
    | *eyápi* | Evidential — "they say" | *Iktómi ohítika eyápi.* = Iktómi was brave, they say. |

### Reported Speech and the Evidential *eyápi*

In oral tradition, it is important to signal that a statement comes from tradition rather than personal witness. The word *eyápi* (they say / it is said) functions as an evidential marker — it acknowledges that the speaker received this knowledge from others:

| Dakota | English |
|--------|---------|
| Eháŋni Iktómi tȟáŋka eyápi. | Long ago, Iktómi was great, they say. |
| Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka wóuŋsipi kȟú eyápi. | The Great Mystery gave wisdom, they say. |
| Waníyetu wóoyake yápi eyápi. | They tell stories in winter, they say. |

## Section 3 — An Iktómi Story Fragment

The following is a simplified teaching narrative in Dakota with interlinear translation. Practice reading it aloud, noting the discourse markers:

---

*Eháŋni Iktómi waŋží tȟáŋka.*
Long ago, Iktómi was great (important to himself).

*Héčhel wakpá ekta yá.*
And so he went to the river.

*Čhokáya šúŋka waŋží wanyáka.*
In the middle, he saw a dog.

*"Mičháŋpá kiŋ lúzahaŋ," eyá.*
"My horse is fast," he said.

*Tká čhaŋpá yuká šni.*
But he had no horse.

*Heháŋ Iktómi įštíŋma.*
Then Iktómi fell asleep.

*Šúŋka kiŋ čhaŋpá kiŋ kiglú.*
The dog took the horse.

*Iktómi kičhí kísapa.*
Iktómi was fooled.

*Oháŋ.*
(So it is / such is the story.)

---

!!! note "Grammar: Story-Closing with *Oháŋ*"
    The word *oháŋ* can open a story (it is said / once) and can also close it — a simple affirmation that the story is complete. This dual use mirrors the circular structure of many Dakota narratives, which end where they begin, or end with a return to the present moment after the narrative journey.

### Key Vocabulary: Ceremony in Narrative Context

| Dakota | Pronunciation | English | Notes |
|--------|---------------|---------|-------|

| yuhápi | yoo-HAH-pee | they hold / they have | Used for ceremony holding |
| wóčhekiye oháŋkapi | woh-CHEH-kee-yeh oh-HAHN-kah-pee | they offer prayers | |
| waŋwíčhayakapi | wahn-WEE-chah-yah-kah-pee | they watch them / they witness | |
| čhantéčhilapi | chahn-TEH-chee-lah-pee | they love (plural) | Closing blessing in ceremony |

!!! info "AIS 112 Connection: Oral Tradition as Knowledge Sovereignty"
    In contemporary Dakota communities, the revitalization of oral tradition is understood as an act of knowledge sovereignty — the assertion that Dakota knowledge systems are valid, complete, and do not require validation from outside. When Dakota elders tell stories to children in the language, they are not merely preserving a cultural artifact; they are transmitting a complete epistemology — a way of knowing the world. This is why language programs and storytelling initiatives are often connected: without the language, the stories change; without the stories, the language loses its fullest meanings.
