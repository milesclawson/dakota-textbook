# Chapter 5 — Wóčhekiye: Prayer, Values & The Sacred

## Summary

Chapter 5 introduces the vocabulary of Dakota spiritual life — prayer, sacred values, and core ethical concepts. Students learn the phrase *Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ* (all my relations), the four cardinal virtues of Dakota life, and vocabulary for ceremony and the sacred. AIS 112 content examines how Dakota spiritual practice was criminalized under U.S. law and how its suppression was inseparable from the broader dispossession of Dakota people.

## AIS 112 Cultural Context — Weeks 9–11

!!! info "Dakota Spiritual Life and the Four Virtues"
    Traditional Dakota spiritual life was not a separate "religion" compartmentalized from daily activity — it was woven through every aspect of living. The four cardinal virtues — *wačháŋtognaka* (generosity), *ohítika* (bravery), *ksapá* (wisdom), and *čhaŋtéčhila* (love) — were not personal aspirations but communal responsibilities. A person who embodied these virtues did not merely improve themselves; they strengthened the entire tiyóšpaye.

    These virtues were demonstrated publicly through give-aways (*wóhanpi*), acts of protection, council deliberation, and the raising of children. The person who gave away the most was honored more than the person who accumulated the most.

!!! warning "Criminalization of Dakota Ceremony"
    Between 1883 and 1934, the U.S. government enforced the Code of Indian Offenses, which made many Dakota spiritual practices illegal — including the Sun Dance, traditional ceremony, and the practices of medicine people. This was not incidental to colonization; it was deliberate cultural and spiritual assault. Dakota people continued their practices in secret, at great personal risk. The American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 finally restored legal protections, though enforcement has remained inconsistent.

!!! info "Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ"
    The phrase *Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ* — often translated as "all my relations" or "we are all related" — is one of the most widely known expressions from Lakota/Dakota spiritual tradition. It opens and closes ceremony; it is spoken at the end of prayer; it is an acknowledgment that the speaker exists in relationship with all living beings — human, animal, plant, and spirit. It is not a greeting; it is a declaration of relationship.

## Introduction

The word *wóčhekiye* means prayer — but the Dakota concept of prayer is broader than most English speakers expect. *Wóčhekiye* is not only formal petition to a deity. It is the ongoing practice of maintaining relationship with all that is sacred: the land, the ancestors, the living community, and the mystery that animates everything. When a Dakota elder says *Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ*, they are not merely being polite. They are asserting an entire cosmological position: that they exist in web of relationship with all life.

The four virtues — generosity, bravery, wisdom, love — are not passive traits in Dakota culture. They are active practices, demonstrated daily and publicly. The most admired person in a Dakota community was not the wealthiest or the most powerful but the most generous — the one who gave away their own goods so that others would not suffer. *Wačháŋtognaka* is often translated as generosity, but it carries deeper weight: the act of expanding your heart outward, beyond yourself, toward the whole community.

The language of Dakota spirituality is also the language of resistance. When Dakota communities maintained their ceremonies in secret during the years of prohibition, they were not merely preserving cultural traditions — they were refusing the colonial demand that they cease to exist as a people.

| wakan | wah-KAHN | sacred, mysterious, powerful | Encompasses divine, spirit, mystery |
| Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka | wah-KHAHN THAHN-kah | Great Spirit / Great Mystery | *tȟáŋka* = great/large |
| wóčhekiye | woh-CHEH-kee-yeh | prayer; the act of praying | |
| Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ | mee-TAH-koo-yeh oh-YAH-sihn | all my relations / we are all related | Ceremonial phrase |
| tȟuŋkášila | thun-KAH-shee-lah | grandfather; Great Spirit (prayer) | Also kinship term |
| čheyáka | cheh-YAH-kah | sage | Used in smudging/purification |
| čhaŋšášayapi | chahn-SHAH-shah-yah-pee | red willow / kinnikinnick | Sacred smoking mixture |
| inípi | ee-NEE-pee | sweat lodge ceremony | Purification ritual |
| wóhanpi | woh-HAHN-pee | feast, give-away | Community generosity ritual |
| čháŋšaša | CHAHN-shah-shah | red willow bark | Ceremonial smoking material |

### Morpheme Analysis: Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Break down the name for the Great Spirit</summary>

| Morpheme | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Wakȟáŋ | sacred, mysterious, beyond ordinary comprehension |
| Tȟáŋka | great, large, vast |

**Analysis:** *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* is often translated as "Great Spirit" in English, but this translation misses something important. *Wakȟáŋ* does not simply mean "spiritual" — it means *mysterious, beyond ordinary knowing, charged with power*. The word is used for electricity, for the steam locomotive (which early Dakota people called *mázawakȟáŋ* — mysterious iron), for sacred ceremony. *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* is therefore not just a "great spirit" but the Great Mystery — the ultimate unknowable source of all that is charged with power. This is a more philosophically precise (and humbler) translation.

</details>

### Morpheme Analysis: Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Break down "all my relations"</summary>

| Morpheme | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| mi- | my / mine (first person possessive) |
| -tákuye | relations, relatives (*takuya* = to be related to) |
| oyásʼiŋ | all, every one |

**Analysis:** The full phrase is *Mi-tákuye Oyásʼiŋ* — literally "all those related to me" or "all my relations." The *mi-* possessive is crucial: this is not "all relations" in general, but *my* relations — a personal declaration of belonging. The speaker claims kinship with everything: human, animal, plant, and spirit. This is the Dakota cosmological statement that the self is not separate from the world but woven into it.

</details>

## Section 2 — The Four Cardinal Virtues

!!! note "Grammar: Stative Verbs for Virtues"
    The four cardinal virtues are expressed as stative verbs in Dakota. To say someone *is* generous, brave, wise, or loving, you use the stative verb conjugated for person:

    | Virtue | Dakota (stative verb) | I am... | You are... |
    |--------|----------------------|---------|------------|
    | Generosity | wačháŋtognaka | wačháŋtognaka waúŋ | wačháŋtognaka yaúŋ |
    | Bravery | ohítika | ohítika waúŋ | ohítika yaúŋ |
    | Wisdom | ksapá | ksapá waúŋ | ksapá yaúŋ |
    | Love / belovedness | čhaŋtéčhila | čhaŋtéčhila waúŋ | čhaŋtéčhila yaúŋ |

### Key Vocabulary: The Four Virtues

| Dakota | Pronunciation | English | Cultural Note |
|--------|---------------|---------|---------------|
| wačháŋtognaka | wah-CHAHN-tog-nah-kah | generosity | Lit. "to place in the heart outward" — to expand generosity |
| ohítika | oh-HEE-tee-kah | bravery, courage | Not recklessness — deliberate courage in service of community |
| ksapá | ksah-PAH | wisdom | Practical wisdom earned through experience and listening |
| čhaŋtéčhila | chahn-TEH-chee-lah | love, cherishing | Lit. "heart-small/dear" — to hold something dear in the heart |

### Example Sentences: Virtues

| Dakota | English |
|--------|---------|
| Ohítika waúŋ kte. | I will be brave. |
| Wičhášta kiŋ ksapá. | The man is wise. |
| Wačháŋtognaka kiŋ waúŋ. | I am generous. (I practice generosity.) |
| Uŋčí kiŋ čhaŋtéčhila. | Grandmother is full of love. |
| Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ. | All my relations. (Ceremonial closing.) |

## Section 3 — Ceremony and Material Culture

### Key Vocabulary: Ceremonial Objects and Practice

| Dakota | Pronunciation | English | Notes |
|--------|---------------|---------|-------|
| čheyáka | cheh-YAH-kah | sage | Burned for purification/smudging |
| čhaŋšášayapi | chahn-SHAH-shah-yah-pee | kinnikinnick / red willow bark | Sacred smoking mixture |
| inípi | ee-NEE-pee | sweat lodge / purification ceremony | *iní* = to take a sweat bath |
| čhanúŋpa | chahn-OON-pah | sacred pipe | The pipe is a prayer vehicle |
| wiwáŋyag wačhípi | wee-WAHN-yahg wah-CHEE-pee | Sun Dance | Lit. "dance looking at the sun" |
| wóuŋsipi | woh-OON-see-pee | learning, practice | From Chapter 3 — repeated here in spiritual context |

!!! info "AIS 112 Connection: The Suppression of Ceremony"
    The *inípi* (sweat lodge) and the *wiwáŋyag wačhípi* (Sun Dance) were specifically targeted by the 1883 Code of Indian Offenses. Violation could result in imprisonment, withholding of rations, and other punishments. Yet Dakota and Lakota communities continued these practices — often in secret, often at night — because they understood that without ceremony, the community could not survive. The revival of these practices after 1978 (American Indian Religious Freedom Act) was therefore not a nostalgic recovery but a continuation of resistance that had never fully stopped.

### Example Sentences: Ceremony

| Dakota | English |
|--------|---------|
| Čheyáka kiŋ yuhápi. | They have sage. |
| Wóčhekiye waúŋ kte. | I will pray. |
| Inípi kiŋ híŋhaŋna. | The sweat lodge (ceremony) is in the morning. |
| Čhanúŋpa kiŋ wakan. | The pipe is sacred. |
| Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ echámuŋ kte. | I will say "all my relations." |
