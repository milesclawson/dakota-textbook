    ??? answer "Answer"
        **b) Heart**

        *čhaŋté* means heart and is the center of Dakota selfhood — courage, love, and grief are located here, not in the head. It appears in many compound words: *čhaŋtéčhila* (love/beloved), *čhantéčhilapi* (we love you).

??? question "2. A female Dakota speaker uses which word to refer to her younger sister?"
    ??? answer "Answer"
        **a) čhuwítkala**

        *čhuwítkala* is the term a female speaker uses for her (younger) sister. A male speaker uses *čhaŋkší* for his sister. This speaker-gender distinction is fundamental to Dakota kinship vocabulary.

    a) čhuwítkala
    b) čhaŋkší
    c) šičhéši
    d) haŋká

??? question "3. What does *čhaŋté mayázaŋ* mean?"
    ??? answer "Answer"
        **c) My heart aches / I am grieving**

        *čhaŋté* = heart; *ma-yázaŋ* = it hurts me (*ma-* = me, *yázaŋ* = to ache). The construction places the body part as the grammatical subject and the experiencer as an object prefix on the verb. It expresses both physical heart pain and emotional grief.

    a) Your heart is strong
    b) The heart is red
    c) My heart aches / I am grieving
    d) I have no heart

??? question "4. The Dakota word *pežúta* primarily means:"
    ??? answer "Answer"
        **b) Medicine / medicinal plant**

        *pežúta* means medicine or medicinal herb — literally it is related to the word for grass/plant, emphasizing that traditional Dakota medicine came from plant knowledge. *pežúta wiŋyaŋ* = medicine woman; *wičhášta wakan* = holy man/medicine man.

    a) Doctor
    b) Medicine / medicinal plant
    c) Sickness
    d) Ceremony

??? question "5. What is the possessive prefix meaning 'my' used with body parts and kinship terms in Dakota?"
    ??? answer "Answer"
        **a) mi- / ma-**

        The first-person possessive prefix is *mi-* (sometimes *ma-* depending on the following vowel and phonological environment). Examples: *mičhaŋté* (my heart), *minape* (my hand), *mičhuwítkala* (my sister, female speaking).

    a) mi- / ma-
    b) ni-
    c) ta-
    d) wa-

??? question "6. What does *šičhéši* mean?"
    ??? answer "Answer"
        **c) Male cousin (cross-cousin)**

        *šičhéši* refers to a male cousin, specifically a cross-cousin in the Dakota kinship system. The cross-cousin relationship (father's sister's child or mother's brother's child) had specific social roles in Dakota society distinct from parallel cousins.

    a) Older brother
    b) Father's brother
    c) Male cousin (cross-cousin)

??? question "7. According to the chapter, why is the body part *čhaŋté* (heart) etymologically connected to the word for 'tree' (*čháŋ*)?"
    ??? answer "Answer"

        *čhaŋté* contains the root *čháŋ* (tree/wood) and implies the innermost core — the "heartwood." Just as the strength of a tree lies in its inner rings, a person's strength comes from their *čhaŋté*. This arboreal metaphor reflects the Dakota world of northern forests and plains.

    a) The heart pumps blood like sap in a tree
    b) The heart is understood as the innermost core, like the heartwood of a tree
    c) Trees were used to make medicine for heart conditions
    d) Both words share a sound but have no etymological connection

??? question "8. Which term is used to address a grandmother respectfully in Dakota?"
    ??? answer "Answer"
        **c) uŋčí**

        *uŋčí* is the Dakota word for grandmother and term of respectful direct address. *tȟuŋkášila* = grandfather (and also used for the Great Spirit in prayer). *até* = father; *thimá* = mother.

    a) tȟuŋkášila
    b) até

??? question "9. To say 'I am well' in Dakota, you would say:"

    b) Waníčiŋ waúŋ.
    c) Pežúta waúŋ.

## Part B — Fill in the Blank (3 questions, 5 points each)

??? question "11. The Dakota word for 'eye' is __________, and 'my eye' is __________."
    ??? answer "Answer"
        - Eye: **išta**
        - My eye: **míšta** (or *miišta*)

        The possessive is formed with *mi-* prefix: *mi-* + *išta* = *míšta*. When the noun begins with a vowel, the *mi-* prefix often contracts: *míšta*, *mí* (my mouth, from *í*).

??? question "12. A male speaker refers to his sister as __________, while a female speaker uses __________ for the same relationship."
    ??? answer "Answer"
        - Male speaker: **čhaŋkší**
        - Female speaker: **čhuwítkala**

        This is one of the most important kinship distinctions in this chapter. The difference reflects not just biological relation but social role: a man's sister occupies a different relational position in his life than a woman's sister occupies in hers.

??? question "13. In the pain construction, *naté niyázaŋ*, the prefix *ni-* means __________ and the overall phrase means __________."
    ??? answer "Answer"
        - *ni-* = **you / your** (second person object prefix)
        - Phrase meaning: **Your head hurts / Do you have a headache?**

        *naté* = head; *ni-yázaŋ* = it hurts you. The verb *yázaŋ* takes an object-position person prefix to indicate the experiencer of the pain.

Student connects this to the precision of Dakota kinship terms (speaker-gender distinctions, cross-cousin vs. parallel cousin distinctions, etc.) and explains why English's simpler kinship vocabulary cannot capture the same social information.

        **Partial credit (5–7 pts):** Student describes kinship as important but does not explain the structural connection between social organization and terminological complexity.

        **Sample answer:** "*Wótakuye* is the Dakota concept of kinship as total social relationship — it determines your obligations, your alliances, your spiritual connections, and your identity. Because kinship organizes everything, the language needs precise vocabulary to encode all the relevant distinctions. English 'sister' is one word because English society doesn't require knowing, from the word itself, who is speaking. Dakota requires this because the social weight of the relationship differs by the speaker's position: a man's obligation to his sister is different from a woman's obligation to hers, and the language encodes this distinction in the words themselves."

??? question "15. Describe the *mayázaŋ* construction in Dakota grammar. How does this construction differ from English expressions of pain, and what does it reveal about Dakota grammatical priorities?"
    ??? answer "Rubric / Sample Answer"
        **Full credit (10 pts):** Student accurately describes the construction ([body part] + [person prefix + yázaŋ]), contrasts it with English ("my head hurts" vs. "I have a headache"), and reflects on what it means to treat the body part as the grammatical subject acting upon the experiencer.

        **Partial credit (5–7 pts):** Student describes the construction correctly but does not reflect on grammatical implications.

        **Sample answer:** "In English, we say 'I have a headache' or 'my head hurts' — the person is usually the subject. In Dakota, the *mayázaŋ* construction places the body part as the grammatical subject and the experiencer as an object prefix on the verb: *Naté mayázaŋ* = 'The head hurts me / My head hurts.' The body part does the acting; the person receives the experience. This reflects a Dakota grammatical tendency to distribute agency — the head is not merely passive; it is *doing* something to me. This construction appears consistently in health vocabulary and reveals how Dakota grammar encodes a relational, intersubjective view of the body."

??? question "16. How did U.S. colonial policies — specifically the boarding school system — target Dakota kinship as a tool of cultural erasure? Connect your answer to at least two specific vocabulary items from this chapter."
    ??? answer "Rubric / Sample Answer"
        **Full credit (10 pts):** Student accurately describes the boarding school system's role in separating children from tiyóšpaye, forbidding Dakota language, and thereby severing the transmission of kinship vocabulary. Student references at least two specific terms (e.g., *čhuwítkala*, *uŋčí*, *tȟuŋkášila*, *haŋká*, etc.) and explains how losing these words meant losing the relationships they named.

        **Partial credit (5–7 pts):** Student identifies the boarding school system and language prohibition but makes only general reference to kinship vocabulary.

        **Sample answer:** "The boarding school system — which removed Dakota children from their communities beginning in the 1870s and intensifying after 1880 — explicitly forbade children from speaking Dakota. Children who arrived still knowing words like *uŋčí* (grandmother) or *čhuwítkala* (younger sister, female speaking) were punished for using them. Within a generation, children returned home unable to address their own grandmothers correctly — they had lost not just words but the relational knowledge those words encoded. *Uŋčí* is not just a label; it is a set of responsibilities, stories, and prayers that flow between a grandmother and grandchild. Losing *uŋčí* meant losing access to that entire relational universe. The same is true of terms like *tȟuŋkášila* (grandfather), which also appears in sacred prayer — to lose it was to lose access to spiritual tradition. This is why linguistic revitalization is inseparable from cultural healing."
