# Chapter 4 — Čhaŋté: Body, Health & Relationships

## Summary

Chapter 4 introduces Dakota vocabulary for body parts, health, emotions, and kinship. Students encounter one of Dakota's most complex and culturally significant systems: the kinship terminology, which varies by the speaker's gender and encodes relationship through social role rather than biological category alone. AIS 112 content addresses Dakota family structures, the role of kinship in community survival, and how the destruction of kinship networks was central to colonial policies targeting Dakota people.

## AIS 112 Cultural Context — Weeks 7–9

!!! info "Kinship as the Foundation of Dakota Society"
    For Dakota people, kinship (*wótakuye*) is not merely a family tree — it is the organizing principle of all social, political, and spiritual life. Before you speak to someone, you establish relationship. Every Dakota social interaction begins with knowing: who are you to me, and who am I to you? This is why Dakota kinship terminology is so precise — it must carry the weight of social organization.

    The kinship system distinguishes not just uncle from aunt, but *which* uncle or aunt, from whose side, and crucially: who is speaking. A woman and a man use different words for "sister" because sisterhood is experienced differently depending on gender role. This is not sexism — it is precision about the social weight of a relationship.

!!! warning "Colonial Disruption of Kinship"
    One of the most devastating effects of the U.S.-Dakota War of 1862, the subsequent forced removal, and the allotment era was the systematic destruction of kinship networks. Children removed to boarding schools were forbidden from speaking Dakota and were cut off from the extended family structures that transmitted the language. The loss of kinship vocabulary — the very words for grandmother, cousin, sister — was therefore not incidental to colonization; it was one of its primary instruments. Revitalizing this vocabulary is an act of cultural restoration.

!!! info "The Body as Sacred Geography"
    In Dakota thought, the body (*čhaŋté* — heart, the core of selfhood) is a sacred geography. The heart is the center, not the head. Wisdom, courage, love — these qualities are not cognitive but cardiac, rooted in the heart. This is why *čhaŋté* appears in so many compound words: *čhaŋtéčhila* (love, "heart-small/dear"), *čhantéčhilapi* (we love you), *čhantéyapi* (beloved).

## Introduction

In English, the word "sister" is one word. In Dakota, it depends on who is speaking. A woman speaking of her sister uses *čhuwítkala*. A man speaking of his sister uses *čhaŋkší*. These are not the same word — they encode two different social relationships, two different sets of responsibilities and emotional weights.

This is the nature of Dakota kinship terminology: it is not a labeling system but a relational system. Every kinship term encodes the speaker's position as well as the referent's position. It tells you not just who the person is, but who they are *to you*, and who you are *to them*.

The body, in Dakota, is understood through the same relational lens. The word *čhaŋté* — heart — is not just the organ that pumps blood. It is the seat of the self, the center of courage, love, and grief. When you learn Dakota body vocabulary, you learn a map of what Dakota culture considers essential in a human being.

### Morpheme Analysis: čhaŋté

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Break down the word for "heart"</summary>

| Morpheme | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| čhaŋ- | from *čháŋ* (wood/tree) — the inner core |
| -té | intensifier; the essential/innermost part |

**Analysis:** *čhaŋté* — heart — carries the sense of the *inner core*, like the heartwood of a tree. Just as a tree's strength comes from its innermost rings, a person's strength comes from their *čhaŋté*. This is why the heart is the center of Dakota selfhood — not the brain, not the hands, but the living core. The metaphor is arboreal: Dakota people, who lived in the forests and prairies of Minnesota, drew their image of interior strength from the trees around them.

</details>

### Possessive Body Part Constructions

## Section 2 — Health and Wellness

### Key Vocabulary: Health

| Dakota | Pronunciation | English | Notes |
|--------|---------------|---------|-------|
| wayázaŋka | wah-YAH-zahn-kah | to be sick | Stative verb |
| waníčiŋ | wah-NEE-chihn | to be well, healthy | |
| mayázaŋ | mah-YAH-zahn | I am sick / it hurts me | *ma-* = me + *yázaŋ* = to hurt/ache |
| niyázaŋ | nee-YAH-zahn | you are sick / it hurts you | |
| pežúta | peh-ZHOO-tah | medicine, herb | Lit. "grass" — plant medicine |
| pežúta wiŋyaŋ | peh-ZHOO-tah WIHN-yahn | medicine woman | |
| wičhášta wakan | wee-CHAH-shtah WAH-kahn | holy man / medicine man | |
| asniwičhayapi | as-nee-WEE-chah-yah-pee | they heal them | |

!!! note "Grammar: The Verb of Pain — mayázaŋ"
    *yázaŋ* is a verb meaning "to ache, to hurt, to be painful." It takes an object prefix to indicate who is experiencing the pain:

    - *Čhaŋté mayázaŋ.* → My heart aches. (I am sad / grieving)
    - *Naté niyázaŋ he?* → Is your head hurting? (Do you have a headache?)
    - *Síha čhiyázaŋ.* → Your foot hurts.

    This construction models an important Dakota grammatical pattern: the body part is the subject, and the person experiencing pain takes an object-position prefix on the verb.

### Example Sentences: Health

| Dakota | English |
|--------|---------|
| Čhaŋté mayázaŋ. | My heart aches. (I am grieving.) |
| Naté niyázaŋ he? | Does your head hurt? |
| Waníčiŋ waúŋ. | I am well. |
| Wayázaŋka waúŋ šni. | I am not sick. |
| Pežúta kiŋ yúta kte. | He/she will take the medicine. |

## Section 3 — Kinship Terms

!!! info "AIS 112 Connection: Kinship and Identity"
    In traditional Dakota society, you introduced yourself by naming your tiyóšpaye and your kin relationships — not your individual name first. Kinship terms were the vocabulary of identity. When missionaries and government officials began recording Dakota names in the 1800s, they often recorded only individual names, erasing the kinship layer that gave those names their social meaning.

### Key Vocabulary: Kinship (Female Speaker)

| Dakota | Pronunciation | English | Notes |
|--------|---------------|---------|-------|
| čhuwítkala | choo-WIHT-kah-lah | younger sister (female speaking) | *-kala* = dear/little |
| čhaŋkší | chahn-KSIH | sister (male speaking) | Different word for same relationship |
| čhiŋkší | chihn-KSIH | son | Used by either parent |
| čhuŋkší | chuhn-KSIH | daughter | |
| thimá | thee-MAH | mother | Vocative/direct address |
| até | ah-TEH | father | Vocative/direct address |
| uŋčí | uhn-CHEE | grandmother | Term of respect/address |
| tȟuŋkášila | thun-KAH-shee-lah | grandfather | Also: Great Spirit (in prayer) |
| haŋká | HAHN-kah | aunt (father's sister) | |
| šičhéši | shee-CHEH-shee | male cousin (cross-cousin) | |

### Kinship: Gender of Speaker Matters

| Relationship | Female Speaker Says | Male Speaker Says |
|-------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| sister | čhuwítkala | čhaŋkší |
| brother | čhiŋkší | čhaŋkší → šičhéši (cousin/brother) |
| friend (female) | kičhíyena | — |
| elder | (respectful address varies) | (respectful address varies) |

!!! note "Grammar: Possessive Kinship Terms"
    Kinship terms take the same possessive prefixes as body parts:

    - *mi-čhuwítkala* → my (younger) sister (female speaking)
    - *ni-čhuwítkala* → your sister
    - *ta-čhuwítkala* → her sister

    In Dakota speech, kinship terms are frequently used without possessive prefixes when the context is clear — "čhuwítkala hí" can mean "my sister arrived" if the speaker is female and context implies it.

### Example Sentences: Kinship

| Dakota | English |
|--------|---------|
| Mičhuwítkala hí. | My (younger) sister arrived. (female speaking) |
| Tȟuŋkášila waŋkáyela. | Grandfather is elderly/respected. |
| Uŋčí kiŋ wóyute wayúta. | Grandmother is eating the food. |
| Šičhéši kiŋ wayáwa tí yá kte. | My male cousin will go to school. |
| Haŋká kiŋ pežúta kiŋ kȟú. | Aunt gave the medicine. |

## Practice Exercises

!!! example "Exercise 1: Body Map"
    Draw a simple outline of a human figure (or use a blank paper). Label at least eight body parts in Dakota. For each, write a possessive phrase using the *mi-* (my) prefix.

    For example: draw an eye, label it *išta*, and write *míšta* (my eye).

!!! example "Exercise 2: Health Sentences"
    Translate these Dakota sentences into English, paying careful attention to the *mayázaŋ* construction:

    1. *Čhaŋté niyázaŋ he?*
    2. *Síha mayázaŋ.*
    3. *Waníčiŋ waúŋ.*
    4. *Pežúta kiŋ wayúta kte šni.*

!!! example "Exercise 3: Kinship Reflection"
    Answer these questions in full Dakota sentences (use vocabulary from this chapter):
    1. Who is your *uŋčí* to you? Write: *Uŋčí kiŋ...* and complete with something she does (use verbs from Chapter 3).
    2. If you are female, how do you say "my younger sister came"?
    3. What is the Dakota word for the person your culture calls "aunt" (father's sister)?
