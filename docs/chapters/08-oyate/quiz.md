??? question "12. The Dakota phrase meaning 'The Dakota language lives' is: Dakota iápi kiŋ __________."
    ??? answer "Answer"
        **wičhoni**

        *Dakota iápi kiŋ **wičhoni**.* — The Dakota language lives. *wičhoni* = life, living. This phrase is used in language revitalization contexts as an affirmation of the language's survival and ongoing presence.

??? question "13. The Seven Council Fires are collectively known in Dakota as __________."
    ??? answer "Answer"
        **Očhéthi Šakówiŋ**

        *Očhéthi* = council fire/hearth; *šakówiŋ* = seven. The Očhéthi Šakówiŋ comprises the seven related council groups of the Dakota (*šakówiŋ* = seven), Nakota, and Lakota peoples. All seven share the Lakȟótiyapi/Dakhótiyapi language family.

??? question "15. Explain the meaning of *wokiksuye* — remembrance — and describe the *Wokiksuye* ride. Why is this act of annual commemoration also an act of political sovereignty?"
    ??? answer "Rubric / Sample Answer"
        **Full credit (10 pts):** Student accurately breaks down *wokiksuye* morphologically (or describes its meaning), describes the annual 330-mile ride from Lower Brule to Mankato, and articulates why annual commemoration is a political act — asserting continuity, refusing erasure, making visible the connection between the living and the 38.

        **Partial credit (5–7 pts):** Accurate description of the ride but shallow analysis of its political dimension.

        **Sample answer:** "*Wokiksuye* breaks down as *wo-* (nominalizer) + *-kik-* (reflexive/reciprocal) + *-suye* (to remember). It is a collective self-remembering — the community remembering itself through its dead. Every December since 1987, riders travel approximately 330 miles from Lower Brule, South Dakota to Mankato, Minnesota, arriving near the anniversary of the December 26, 1862 executions. The ride is an act of political sovereignty because it refuses the erasure that the U.S. government attempted in 1862 and after. To say 'we remember' is to assert that the connection between the living Dakota people and the 38 men is unbroken — that the executions did not end the Dakota nation, that the exile did not sever the people from their history. The ride makes this assertion physically, publicly, and annually. It is not nostalgia; it is witness. And it takes place on the land — on the *makóčhe* — from which the Dakota people were expelled, demonstrating that the relationship to the land also continues."
