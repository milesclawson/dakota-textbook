# Chapter 8 — Oyáte: Nation, Treaties & Sovereignty

## Summary

Chapter 8 introduces the vocabulary of Dakota nationhood, political identity, and sovereignty. Students learn the terminology for the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ (Seven Council Fires), treaty history, contemporary Dakota communities, and the language of political self-determination. AIS 112 content provides a comprehensive treatment of the U.S.-Dakota War of 1862, the mass execution of 38 Dakota men in Mankato, the Fort Snelling internment camp, and the ongoing work of Dakota sovereignty today.

## AIS 112 Cultural Context — Weeks 15–16 (Final Unit)

!!! info "Očhéthi Šakówiŋ — The Seven Council Fires"
    The Očhéthi Šakówiŋ (often written Oceti Sakowin) — the Seven Council Fires — is the traditional political and cultural confederation of the Dakota, Nakota, and Lakota peoples. The name *Očhéthi Šakówiŋ* means "Seven (Council) Fires" (*očhéthi* = fireplace/hearth, *šakówiŋ* = seven). Each fire represents one of the seven council groups:

    1. **Bdewákanthuŋwaŋ** (Mdewakanton) — People of the Spirit Lake
    2. **Wahpékute** — Shooters Among the Leaves
    3. **Sisíthuŋwaŋ** (Sisseton) — People of the Swampy Land
    4. **Wahpéthuŋwaŋ** (Wahpeton) — People of the Leaf Village
    5. **Iháŋkthuŋwaŋ** (Yankton) — People of the End Village
    6. **Iháŋkthuŋwaŋna** (Yanktonai) — Little People of the End Village
    7. **Thítȟuŋwaŋ** (Teton/Lakota) — People of the Prairie

    The Dakota people are primarily the four eastern fires (1–4). All seven share the Lakȟótiyapi / Dakhótiyapi language family.

!!! warning "The U.S.-Dakota War of 1862 and Its Aftermath"
    The U.S.-Dakota War of 1862 — called *Čhaŋtéšiča* (heartbreak/sorrow) in Dakota — was the deadliest conflict on Minnesota soil. It followed a decade of treaty violations, withheld annuity payments, and starvation on the reservation. In August 1862, Dakota warriors led by Little Crow (*Tȟašúŋke Čhokípa* — His Horse Is Feared) attacked U.S. settlements along the Minnesota River.

    After the war, the U.S. government's response was catastrophic:
    - **Military tribunal:** 303 Dakota men were sentenced to death in trials lasting as little as five minutes.
    - **Lincoln's review:** President Lincoln commuted most sentences, leaving 38 men condemned.
    - **December 26, 1862:** The 38 Dakota men were hanged simultaneously in Mankato, Minnesota — the largest mass execution in U.S. history.
    - **Fort Snelling internment:** Approximately 1,700 Dakota women, children, and elderly were imprisoned in a concentration camp below Fort Snelling during the winter of 1862–63. Dozens died of disease and exposure.
    - **Exile and bounty:** The Dakota people were expelled from Minnesota; the state placed a bounty on Dakota scalps. Survivors were exiled to Crow Creek Reservation in South Dakota, where hundreds more died.

!!! warning "The 38 + 2: Ongoing Commemoration"
    Every December, the Dakota community commemorates the Mankato executions with the *Wokiksuye* (remembrance/memorial) ride — a 330-mile horseback ride from Lower Brule, South Dakota to Mankato, Minnesota. This ride has taken place annually since 1987. The "38 + 2" designation refers to the 38 men hanged and two others who were executed later. The commemoration is not merely historical — it is an act of living memory and political witness.

!!! info "Contemporary Dakota Communities"
    Dakota people live today in numerous communities across Minnesota, South Dakota, Nebraska, North Dakota, Canada (Manitoba and Saskatchewan), and beyond. In Minnesota, the four Mdewakanton communities — Prairie Island, Shakopee Mdewakanton Sioux Community, Upper Sioux Community, and Lower Sioux Community — have worked for generations on language revitalization, economic development, and the recovery of sacred sites including Bdóte.

## Introduction

The word *oyáte* means nation, people, community. It is the word the Dakota use to speak of themselves as a collective: *Dakota oyáte* — the Dakota people, the Dakota nation. The word does not mean "tribe" — that term, with its implications of primitiveness and smallness, was imposed from outside. *Oyáte* is a word of full political weight: it names a sovereign people with their own governance, their own land, their own laws, and their own relationships to the world.

The Dakota oyáte entered the nineteenth century as the inheritors of a vast, living homeland — Mní Sota Makhóčhe — and emerged from it with their homeland taken, their leaders hanged, their women and children imprisoned, and their people scattered across the continent. This is not ancient history. There are Dakota people living today whose great-grandparents survived the Fort Snelling concentration camp, the Crow Creek exile, and the bounty years. The language that this textbook teaches was spoken by those survivors; it was forbidden to their children; it is being reclaimed by their grandchildren and great-grandchildren.

Learning Dakota is not a neutral academic exercise. It is participation in the ongoing story of a people who have refused to disappear.

### Morpheme Analysis: Očhéthi Šakówiŋ

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Break down the name of the Seven Council Fires</summary>

| Morpheme | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Očhéthi | fireplace, hearth, council fire |
| Šakówiŋ | seven |

**Analysis:** The Seven Council Fires are named for the *očhéthi* — the hearth fire that is both literally and metaphorically the center of a council gathering. In Dakota governance, a council fire is not merely a meeting — it is the living symbol of the community's continuity. Each of the seven fires represents a distinct group with its own territory, dialect, and council, but all share kinship and the obligation of mutual aid. The name *Očhéthi Šakówiŋ* is both a political designation and a kinship declaration: we are seven fires who share one fire's warmth.

</details>

### Morpheme Analysis: wokiksuye

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Break down the word for "remembrance"</summary>

| Morpheme | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| wo- | nominalizing prefix |
| -kik- | reflexive/reciprocal infix |
| -suye | to remember, to think of |

**Analysis:** *wokiksuye* — remembrance — is the word used for the annual December ride commemorating the 38 men hanged at Mankato. The reflexive infix *-kik-* makes this a collective self-remembering: the community remembering itself through its dead. This is not nostalgia or grief alone — it is a political act of self-continuity. The ride asserts: we are still here, we still remember, the connection between the living and the 38 is unbroken.

</details>

### Key Vocabulary: Sovereignty and Today

| Dakota | Pronunciation | English | Notes |
|--------|---------------|---------|-------|
| wóokihi | woh-OH-kee-hee | ability, power, sovereignty | The capacity to act for oneself |
| wóičhaŋkuŋ | woh-ee-CHAHN-kuhn | revival, restoration, revitalization | Language revitalization |
| wóuŋsipi wíčhoni | woh-OON-see-pee WEE-choh-nee | living learning / education for life | Contemporary language program concept |
| oyáte wičhóni | oh-YAH-teh wee-CHOH-nee | the people live / the nation lives | |
| Dakota iápi | dah-KOH-tah ee-AH-pee | Dakota language | *iápi* = language, speech |
| wičhoni | WEE-choh-nee | life, living | |

!!! info "Contemporary Dakota Language Revitalization"
    Today, Dakota language revitalization programs operate at all four Minnesota Dakota communities (Prairie Island, Shakopee, Upper Sioux, Lower Sioux) as well as in Sisseton-Wahpeton, Standing Rock, Crow Creek, and among diaspora communities in Canada. Methods range from master-apprentice programs (pairing fluent elders with learners) to immersion schools for children to university courses like this one. Every speaker gained is an assertion of sovereignty. Every child who learns *Háu* or *Haŋ* is continuing a chain of transmission that was nearly broken.
