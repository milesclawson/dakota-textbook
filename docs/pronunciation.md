# Pronunciation Guide — Standard Dakota Orthography

Dakota is written using a Latin-based orthography with accent marks and special characters. The system used in this course is the **Standard Dakota Orthography** used by the Dakota Language Institute and most Minnesota Dakota communities.

---

## Vowels

Dakota has five vowels, each with a short and a stressed (accented) form. Accent marks indicate both stress and a slightly different quality.

| Written | Sounds Like | Example | English |
|---------|-------------|---------|---------|
| `a` | *a* in "father" | ata | his/her body |
| `á` | *a* in "father" (stressed, longer) | máza | metal / iron |
| `e` | *e* in "café" | eya | he/she said |
| `i` | *ee* in "see" | inila | quiet |
| `í` | *ee* in "see" (stressed) | wíčhaša | man |
| `o` | *o* in "go" | oŋ | to use |
| `ú` | *oo* in "moon" (stressed) | ú | to come |

!!! tip "Accent Marks = Stress"
    In Dakota, the accent mark (á, é, í, ó, ú) marks the stressed syllable. Unlike English, where stress is unpredictable, learning Dakota accent placement will significantly improve your pronunciation.

## Consonants — Special Characters

Most Dakota consonants are similar to English. The following require attention:

| Written | Sounds Like | Example | English |
|---------|-------------|---------|---------|
| `č` | *ch* in "church" | čhaŋté | heart |
| `š` | *sh* in "shoe" | šúŋka | dog |
| `ȟ` | *ch* in German "Bach" (back of throat) | ȟaŋhépi | night |
| `ǧ` | voiced version of ȟ (gargled) | waǧáčhaŋ | cottonwood |
| `ŋ` | *ng* in "sing" | waŋží | one |
| `ź` | *zh* like *s* in "measure" | — | (rare) |
| `'` (glottal stop) | brief throat catch, like "uh-oh" | ič'íčaǧa | to make for oneself |

### Aspirated Consonants

Dakota distinguishes plain, aspirated, and ejective consonants — a three-way distinction English does not have:

| Type | Written | Example | Explanation |
|------|---------|---------|-------------|
| Plain | `p`, `t`, `k`, `č` | tópa (four) | like English p/t/k |
| Aspirated | `ph`, `th`, `kh`, `čh` | čhaŋté (heart) | with a strong puff of air |
| Ejective | `p'`, `t'`, `k'`, `č'` | — | sharp, popping sound |

!!! note "Aspirated Consonants Are Common"
    In Dakota, the aspirated forms (*ph*, *th*, *kh*, *čh*) appear frequently. The `h` after the consonant is NOT a separate sound — it marks aspiration. So *čhaŋté* (heart) is one syllable cluster: "chh-aŋ-tay."

## Gender Differences in Speech

Dakota has one of the most well-known gender distinctions in Indigenous North American languages: men and women use different words for greetings and some sentence-final particles.

| Expression | Male Form | Female Form |
|------------|-----------|-------------|
| Hello / greeting | **Háu** | **Haŋ** |
| Yes | **Háŋ** | **Háŋ** (same) |
| Sentence-final affirmation | **-ye** or **-yelo** (male) | **-ye** (female, softer) |

!!! info "Why Gender Distinctions in Language?"
    The male/female speech distinction in Dakota reflects traditional social roles and speech contexts. Today, many Dakota speakers use both forms fluidly, especially in mixed-gender settings. The distinction is not rigid in contemporary use but is important to understand historically and in formal or ceremonial contexts.

## Nasalized Vowels

The character `ŋ` after a vowel nasalizes it (adds an *ng* resonance through the nose). This changes the sound and the meaning:

- *waŋ* = a (article/particle) vs. *wa* = snow
- *oŋ* = to use vs. *o* = to hit/shoot (in some forms)
