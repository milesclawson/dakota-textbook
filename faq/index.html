    Dakhotiyapi — Frequently Asked Questions¶

    Language Content Notice: Vocabulary and grammar examples in this FAQ are AI-assisted and have not yet been reviewed by Dakota-speaking community members. If you notice an error, use the Exit Ticket at the bottom of the relevant chapter to flag it.

    Getting Started¶

    What is this course about?¶

    Dakhotiyapi — Beginning Dakota Language is a full-year high school introductory course that teaches the Dakota language alongside the history, culture, and sovereignty of the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ (Seven Council Fires). Dakota language and Dakota history are taught together: you will not study one without the other. See the Course Description for the full semester structure and learning outcomes.

    Who is this course for?¶

    This course is designed for high school students with no prior knowledge of Dakota or any Indigenous language. It is cross-listed with AIS 112 — American Indian Studies, so students taking it will earn credit in both language and social studies. Dakota heritage learners and students with community connections are warmly welcomed — your background enriches the class.

    Do I need any prior language experience?¶

    No. The only prerequisite is a willingness to engage respectfully with Dakota language and history. Start by reading the Pronunciation Guide before opening Chapter 1 — it introduces the accent marks and special characters you will encounter throughout the course.

    How is the course structured?¶

    The course runs for 160 instructional days divided into two semesters. Chapters 1–4 cover greetings, numbers, daily life, and relationships, leading to a Mid-Year Exam at the ACTFL Novice Low–Mid level. Chapters 5–8 cover values, land, ceremony, and sovereignty, culminating in an End-of-Year Exam at Novice Mid–High. See Course Description for the full chapter map.

    What level of fluency will I reach by the end?¶

    By the end of the course, you will be at approximately ACTFL Intermediate Low — able to produce simple sentences, ask and answer basic questions, and communicate short personal information in Dakota. See the ACTFL Proficiency Scale for detailed descriptors at each level.

    How do I navigate this textbook?¶

    Use the tabs at the top of the page. Each chapter has a Content page and a Quiz. The Exams tab has both the Mid-Year and End-of-Year exams. The Games tab has six interactive vocabulary games. The MicroSims tab has interactive exercises embedded in Chapters 1 and 2. The Glossary and Resources tabs are reference tools you can use anytime.

    How much time should I expect to spend per chapter?¶

    Each chapter is designed for approximately 4 weeks (20 instructional days) of class time. If you are studying independently, expect 8–12 hours of focused study per chapter to complete the reading, exercises, and quiz.

    Is the language content in this course verified by Dakota speakers?¶

    Not yet. The language content was generated with AI assistance and has not yet been reviewed or approved by Dakota language speakers or community language authorities. The historical and cultural AIS 112 content is drawn from established scholarship and is more reliable. See the About page and the disclaimer on the Home page for details. Use the Exit Ticket at the bottom of any chapter to flag errors.

    What does the disclaimer about AI-generated content mean for learners?¶

    It means you should treat vocabulary and grammar examples as a starting point, not a final authority. The structural patterns (SOV word order, stative verb behavior, suffix systems) are well-documented in Dakota linguistics and are reliable. Specific words and pronunciations are the areas where community verification matters most. When in doubt, cross-reference with the Resources page, which lists community-vetted sources.

    How do I report an error in the language content?¶

    Scroll to the bottom of any chapter and use the Exit Ticket widget. Select "Yes — I noticed something that may be incorrect," describe what you found, and submit via email. Reports from Dakota speakers and heritage learners are given the highest weight.

    Core Concepts¶

    What is the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ?¶

    The Očhéthi Šakówiŋ — the Seven Council Fires — is the political and cultural alliance of seven related peoples who share a common language family and worldview. The four eastern fires speak Dakota (Mdewakanton, Wahpekute, Sisseton, Wahpeton), the two middle fires speak Nakota, and the western fire speaks Lakota. This course teaches Dakota, the dialect of the Minnesota communities. See Chapter 1 for the full vocabulary and map.

    What is a tiyóšpaye?¶

    The tiyóšpaye is the extended family band — the basic social and political unit of Dakota society. A tiyóšpaye was a group of related families who camped, hunted, governed, and made decisions together. Dakota identity begins with the tiyóšpaye, not with the individual. When Dakota people introduced themselves, they named their tiyóšpaye and their place within it before they named themselves. See Chapter 1.

    What does "Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ" mean?¶

    Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ is often translated as "All my relations" or "We are all related." It expresses a worldview in which humans are in relationship not only with other people but with animals, plants, land, water, and the cosmos. It is used as a prayer, a greeting, and a closing — an acknowledgment of interconnection. See Chapter 5 for full cultural context.

    What is the difference between Dakota, Lakota, and Nakota?¶

    These are three dialects of the same language family, often called Siouan or Dakhóta. Dakota is spoken by the four eastern Council Fires (Minnesota communities); Nakota by the two middle fires; and Lakota by the Thítȟuŋwaŋ (Teton people) of the northern Great Plains. The dialects are mutually intelligible to varying degrees but differ in vocabulary, sound system, and some grammatical forms. This course teaches Dakota only. See Course Description.

    What are the Seven Dakota Values taught in this course?¶

    Chapter 5 introduces the core values that frame Dakota ethical life. The seven often cited include wačháŋtognaka (generosity), ohítika (bravery), ksapá (wisdom), wóčhekiye (prayer/spirituality), wówauŋšila (compassion), wačhékiyapi (respect), and wóksape (understanding). These are not abstract principles — they are practiced ways of living embedded in language and ceremony. See Chapter 5.

    What happened in the U.S.-Dakota War of 1862?¶

    The U.S.-Dakota War of 1862 was an armed conflict between the Dakota people and the United States government, triggered by treaty violations, withheld annuity payments, and starvation conditions on the restricted reservation. Following the war, 38 Dakota men were executed by hanging in Mankato, Minnesota — the largest mass execution in U.S. history. Hundreds of Dakota women, children, and elders were imprisoned at Fort Snelling. The aftermath included forced exile from Minnesota and land confiscation. See Chapter 8 for full historical context.

    What is the boarding school era and why is it relevant to this course?¶

    From roughly 1880 to 1970, federal policy forced Native children — including Dakota children — into boarding schools where speaking Indigenous languages was punished. The policy explicitly aimed to destroy Indigenous cultures. The result was a catastrophic break in Dakota language transmission across multiple generations. Understanding this history is essential to understanding why language revitalization matters today. See Chapter 7 and Chapter 8.

    What is language revitalization and why does Dakota need it?¶

    Language revitalization is the effort to restore or expand the use of a language that has declined in speakers, often due to colonization or suppression. Dakota is classified as a severely endangered language — the result of boarding school policies, relocation, and legal suppression of ceremony and culture. Revitalization efforts include immersion schools, master-apprentice programs, documentation projects, and courses like this one. See Resources for community organizations involved in this work.

    What is wóoyake?¶

    Wóoyake means story or oral narrative — but in Dakota culture, it carries more weight than the English word "story" suggests. Oral tradition was the primary technology for preserving history, law, ethics, and ecological knowledge across generations. Wóoyake is not entertainment; it is a living archive. The course introduces narrative vocabulary and story structure in Chapter 7.

    What is Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka?¶

    Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka is often translated as "Great Spirit" or "Great Mystery." It refers to the sacred, mysterious, and incomprehensible dimension of existence that Dakota spiritual practice engages with. It is not equivalent to the Christian God, though colonizers often translated it that way. The concept is introduced in Chapter 5.

    What is the waniyetu wowapi (winter count)?¶

    A waniyetu wowapi is a pictographic record kept on bison hide, used to track years by significant events. One keeper per community would select the most memorable event of the year and add a symbol to the count, creating a visual timeline of community history. The Dakota counting system and the winter count are connected in Chapter 2.

    What is the significance of the Bdóte in Dakota history?¶

    Bdóte — the confluence of the Minnesota and Mississippi Rivers near present-day Fort Snelling — is considered the creation place of the Mdewakanton Dakota. It is one of the most sacred sites in Dakota geography. It is also the site of the Fort Snelling internment camp where Dakota people were imprisoned in 1862–63. The historical and sacred significance of land and place is developed in Chapter 6 and Chapter 8.

    Technical Questions — Grammar & Language¶

    What does SOV mean and why does Dakota use it?¶

    SOV stands for Subject-Object-Verb — the word order in which the verb comes at the end of a sentence. English uses SVO (Subject-Verb-Object): "I see the dog." Dakota says the equivalent of "I the dog see." This is not a quirk; SOV is the most common word order among the world's languages. It reflects a different logic of how actions, agents, and objects relate. See Chapter 3 for practice with SOV construction.

    What is a stative verb in Dakota?¶

    A stative verb is a verb that describes a state of being rather than an action. In Dakota, words that English treats as adjectives — colors, sizes, qualities — are stative verbs. "Red" is not a property a horse has; it is a state the horse is in. So you say šúŋka kiŋ šá — "the dog, it-is-red" — rather than placing a color word before the noun. Stative verbs conjugate for person, just like action verbs. See Chapter 2.

    What is the difference between VAI and VT verbs in the glossary?¶

    VAI (Verb Active Intransitive) describes verbs that describe a subject's action but take no object: "to go," "to eat (in general)." VT (Verb Transitive) describes verbs that take a direct object: "to eat (something specific)." Dakota marks this distinction grammatically, and the choice of verb class affects how the sentence is built. See the Glossary for type labels on each entry.

    How does Dakota form the future tense?¶

    The future tense in Dakota is formed by attaching the suffix -kte to the verb stem. For negation, attach -šni. For negative future ("will not"), use -kte-šni in combination. These suffixes are introduced in Chapter 3 and practiced in the Verb Builder game.

    How does negation work in Dakota?¶

    Negation is expressed with the suffix -šni attached to the verb. Mašá šni — "I am not red." Yá šni — "he/she does not go." The suffix follows the verb stem and precedes any sentence-final particles. See Chapter 3 and Chapter 2 for examples.

    How does the name construction work in Dakota?¶

    Instead of "My name is ," Dakota says ___ emáčiyapi — "They call me ." The construction uses a passive form: your name is something that your community gives you and calls you by. To ask someone's name: Táku eníčiyapi he? (What do they call you?). This grammatical structure encodes a cultural value — identity is socially conferred, not self-declared. See Chapter 1.

    What are the gender speech forms in Dakota?¶

    One of Dakota's most distinctive features is that men and women use different forms for the same greeting. Men say Háu; women say Haŋ. This distinction extends to sentence-final particles: male speakers may use -yelo or -lo; female speakers use -ye or softer endings. Both forms are taught in this course so students can recognize and use them appropriately. See Chapter 1.

    Why does Dakota have one word for both blue and green?¶

    Tó covers the range of color that English splits into blue and green. Many languages in the world — Japanese ao, Irish glas, Welsh glas — similarly use a single term for what English treats as two distinct colors. Color categories vary across languages and reflect different ways of organizing visual experience. Dakota distinguishes other aspects of color that English collapses (e.g., the difference between sáŋ and sápa for pale/white vs. deep black). See Chapter 2.

    How does the Dakota number system work above ten?¶

    Dakota uses a transparent additive system. Wikčémna = ten. Numbers above ten are built: wikčémna waŋží aké waŋží = "ten, one, and one more" = eleven. Multiples of ten: wikčémna núŋpa = twenty, wikčémna yámni = thirty. One hundred is opáwiŋğe. The logic is fully compositional — once you know 1–10 and the connector aké, you can build any number. Practice with the Number Builder MicroSim.

    What is the diminutive suffix -la in Dakota?¶

    The suffix -la (also -na) signals smallness combined with tenderness or affection. It appears in kinship terms (čhuwítkala = younger sister, female speaking; the -kala adds the diminutive), in terms of endearment, and in some adjectives (čísčila = small/little). Understanding this suffix is essential for reading the kinship vocabulary in Chapter 4.

    What is the Standard Dakota Orthography?¶

    The Standard Dakota Orthography is the writing system used in this course and in contemporary Dakota language materials. It uses the standard Latin alphabet plus special characters: ŋ (nasalized n), č (ch sound), š (sh sound), ž (zh sound), ȟ (deep h), ğ (soft g/gh), ȟ with under-dot for the ejective, and accent marks (acute accent for stress). See the Pronunciation Guide for the full guide.

    What is an evidential particle in Dakota?¶

    An evidential particle marks the source of information — how the speaker knows what they are saying. Eyápi ("they say") marks reported speech or traditional narrative — information received from others rather than witnessed directly. This is important in oral tradition, where speakers signal whether they are reporting what they saw, what they heard, or what has been handed down. See Chapter 7.

    Common Challenges¶

    Why is Dakota pronunciation difficult for English speakers?¶

    Dakota has several sounds that do not exist in English: the uvular ȟ (pronounced far back in the throat), the glottalized kȟ and čh (with a catch in the breath), the nasal ŋ, and the distinction between aspirated and unaspirated stops. Accent marks indicate stress placement, which changes meaning. The best approach is to listen to native recordings and practice one sound at a time. See the Pronunciation Guide.

    I keep confusing Háu and Háŋ — what is the difference?¶

    Háu (HOW) is the male greeting — hello. Haŋ (HON) is the female greeting — hello. Háŋ (HAHN, with a long mark) means "yes." All three look similar in writing but are distinct in sound, length, and tone. A useful memory strategy: Háu sounds like the English exclamation "how!"; Háŋ for yes has that extended vowel length.

    Why does "emáčiyapi" feel so different from "my name is"?¶

    Because it encodes a different relationship between identity and community. English "my name is" treats your name as a possession you declare. Dakota emáčiyapi — "they call me" — treats your name as something given by your community and recognized by others. The grammatical difference reflects a real philosophical difference about the nature of selfhood. This is not harder to learn; it is different to learn. See Chapter 1.

    I keep forgetting to put the verb at the end — how do I practice SOV order?¶

    The Sentence Scramble game is specifically designed for this. It gives you scrambled Dakota words and asks you to drag them into the correct SOV order. The key mental move is to plan your sentence backwards from English: identify the verb first, then the object, then the subject. Practice describing simple things — "The dog is red. The sky is blue." — until the verb-final pattern becomes automatic.

    Are stative verbs conjugated the same way as action verbs?¶

    Stative verbs use personal prefixes rather than the suffixes used in some action verbs. For color verbs: first person prefix is ma-, second person is ni-, third person has no prefix. So: mašá (I am red), ništá (you are red), šá (it is red). This is different from how English adjectives work but consistent within Dakota's grammatical logic. See Chapter 2.

    How do I know which kinship term to use in Dakota?¶

    Dakota kinship is complex because it depends on the speaker's gender and the relative's gender and age. Čhankší = sister (male speaking); čhuwítkala = younger sister (female speaking). There is no single word for "brother" that works for all speakers — the term changes based on who is speaking and what the relationship is. Start with the terms in Chapter 4 and build from there.

    What is the difference between waŋká and kȟáŋta?¶

    Both can translate as "old," but they are not interchangeable. Kȟáŋta describes old objects (an old book, an old moccasin). Waŋká is used for elders — people who are old — and carries respect. Using kȟáŋta for a person would be considered rude. The distinction reflects how Dakota encodes respect for human dignity in the structure of the language itself. See Chapter 2.

    Best Practices for Learners¶

    What is the most effective way to study Dakota vocabulary?¶

    Use the spaced repetition principle: review vocabulary multiple times at increasing intervals rather than cramming once. The Flashcard Drill game builds in a streak multiplier system that rewards sustained recall. Pair each vocabulary item with a context — a sentence, an image, or a cultural fact — rather than memorizing isolated words.

    Should I focus on reading or speaking first?¶

    Both matter, but start with pronunciation before reading. The written form of Dakota is phonetically consistent — once you learn what each character sounds like, the spelling reliably tells you the pronunciation. This means learning to read and learning to speak reinforce each other. Read words aloud as you encounter them. See the Pronunciation Guide.

    How do I practice speaking when there is no one to talk with?¶

    Talk to yourself. Describe what you see: Maȟpíya kiŋ tó (the sky is blue). Count objects in the room. Greet your reflection in the mirror with the correct gendered greeting. Record yourself on your phone and play it back. The goal in this course is Novice-level production — short phrases and memorized patterns — so even solitary practice builds real communicative ability.

    How should I engage with Dakota cultural content as a non-Native learner?¶

    With humility, curiosity, and care. You are learning about living communities with ongoing relationships to this land. Some content — particularly ceremony and sacred knowledge — is restricted and is not taught in this course. The AIS 112 historical content is included so that you understand the context of what you are learning: a language that survived deliberate suppression. Engage with that history honestly. See About for the land acknowledgment.

    How do the MicroSims help with learning?¶

    The MicroSims in Chapters 1 and 2 provide interactive, low-stakes practice in a format the textbook alone cannot offer. The Greeting Selector builds automatic recall of gendered greetings. The Council Fires Network makes the relationships between the seven peoples visual and explorable. The Name Builder lets you construct your own Dakota introduction sentence. Use them after reading the chapter, not instead of it.

    How do the exit tickets work and should I use them?¶

    The Exit Ticket at the bottom of each chapter is a 90-second reflection tool. It asks how confident you feel, whether anything seemed inaccurate, and lets you send a note to the teacher if you noticed a problem. If you have Dakota language background or community knowledge, your accuracy feedback is especially valuable — it directly improves the course for future learners.

    What resources should I use alongside this textbook?¶

    The Resources page lists community-vetted materials. For Dakota specifically: the Dakhóta Iápi Okhódakičhiye (Dakota Language Society) at dakhota.org maintains verified materials and community programs. The Dakota Indian Foundation and the Mni Sota Makoce teaching resources are reliable for historical context. Community verification of language content in this course is ongoing.

    Advanced Topics¶

    What is the relationship between language and sovereignty?¶

    Language is not separate from sovereignty — it is one expression of it. Treaty rights, land rights, and the right to govern according to Dakota law are all connected to the continuity of Dakota cultural life, of which language is a central carrier. The U.S. government understood this: the boarding school system explicitly targeted language as a means of destroying political resistance. Learning why language was suppressed illuminates why revitalization is a political as well as cultural act. See Chapter 8.

    What is the Code of Indian Offenses and how did it affect Dakota language?¶

    The Code of Indian Offenses (1883) was a set of federal regulations that criminalized Native religious ceremonies, dances, and cultural practices. While it did not explicitly ban language, it suppressed the ceremonial and cultural contexts in which Dakota was transmitted — storytelling, ceremony, medicine, song. The Code is discussed in Chapter 7 as part of the history of cultural suppression.

    How does the animate/inanimate distinction in related languages compare to Dakota grammar?¶

    This course focuses on Dakota, which does not use animate/inanimate verb classes the way Ojibwe does (VAI/VTI). However, Dakota does make distinction between noun classes in certain grammatical contexts — notably in demonstratives and definite articles. The Ojibwe animate/inanimate system is discussed in the Ojibwe course if you are studying both.

    What is the ACTFL scale and how is it used to evaluate Dakota proficiency?¶

    The ACTFL (American Council on the Teaching of Foreign Languages) Proficiency Guidelines provide a common framework for describing language ability across all languages. The scale runs from Novice Low through Distinguished. This course targets Novice Low through Intermediate Low. See the ACTFL Proficiency Scale page for descriptors at each level and how they apply to Dakota production.

    How does Dakota oral tradition differ from written history?¶

    Oral tradition (wóoyake) is not simply the spoken equivalent of a written record. It is a living practice of transmission in which knowledge is embodied, relational, and contextual. The teller is responsible to the tradition; the listener is active, not passive. Oral traditions are often epistemically more reliable than written records for events within Indigenous communities — they encode ecological knowledge, law, genealogy, and ethics in forms designed to survive and transmit across generations. Written records of Dakota history were produced largely by Euro-American observers with their own biases. See Chapter 7.

    What does it mean that this course is cross-listed with AIS 112?¶

    AIS 112 — American Indian Studies — provides the historical, political, and cultural context for the language you are learning. Every chapter pairs Dakota language content with AIS 112 material: you will not learn the word for "land" without also learning about the treaties that removed Dakota people from their land. This integration is intentional. Language without history produces misunderstanding; history without language produces distance. The pairing is the course.
